Conceptual Framework Linkage

Disclosing known commitments not yet recognized as liabilities
ASPE Section 3280 requires DISCLOSURE of CONTRACTUAL obligations EXISTING at the BALANCE sheet DATE – EVEN where THOSE obligations are NOT yet recognized AS a LIABILITY within the FINANCIAL statements THEMSELVES. THIS is a NARROW, purely DISCLOSURE-oriented standard, GENUINELY distinct FROM Section 3290'S own TREATMENT of UNCERTAIN, contingent EVENTS (Section 3).
2

Scope

Known, existing contractual commitments
This Section applies to CONTRACTUAL obligations THAT are KNOWN and EXISTING as OF the balance SHEET date – SUCH as long-TERM purchase COMMITMENTS or OTHER non-cancellable CONTRACTUAL arrangements – WHERE the AMOUNT is NOT yet recognized AS a liability BECAUSE the underlying GOODS or SERVICES have NOT yet been RECEIVED.
3

Contractual Obligations vs Contingencies – a Key Distinction

Known commitments, not uncertain future events
A CONTRACTUAL obligation is a KNOWN, EXISTING commitment UNDER a SIGNED contract – THE obligation ITSELF is NOT uncertain, ONLY its RECOGNITION as a LIABILITY is DEFERRED until PERFORMANCE occurs. THIS stands IN clear CONTRAST to SECTION 3290'S own TREATMENT of CONTINGENCIES – UNCERTAIN future EVENTS whose OUTCOME is NOT yet KNOWN (see the COMPANION poster).
4

Common Examples

Long-term commitments not yet reflected in the Balance Sheet

5

What This Section Does NOT Require

No liability recognition, and no uncertain contingency disclosure
THIS Section does NOT require RECOGNIZING a LIABILITY for THE contractual OBLIGATION itself (THAT occurs, IF at ALL, only WHEN performance UNDER the CONTRACT actually OCCURS, or under OTHER specific ASPE Sections), and DOES NOT address UNCERTAIN, contingent MATTERS, WHICH remain GOVERNED separately BY Section 3290.
6

Materiality Threshold

Only significant obligations warrant disclosure
CONSISTENT with the GENERAL materiality CONCEPT introduced IN Section 1000, ONLY contractual OBLIGATIONS that ARE genuinely SIGNIFICANT to a READER'S understanding OF the enterprise'S FUTURE cash REQUIREMENTS warrant DISCLOSURE – ROUTINE, immaterial COMMITMENTS (e.g. a SHORT-term office SUPPLY contract) do NOT require SPECIFIC disclosure UNDER this Section.
7

Worked Example – Disclosing a Long-Term Purchase Commitment

A known, existing commitment, disclosed but not yet a liability
Example: A take-or-pay supply agreement
A manufacturing company signs a 5-year take-or-pay raw materials supply agreement, committing to purchase a minimum of $600,000 of materials per year, regardless of actual usage.
The COMPANY discloses the CONTRACTUAL obligation – $600,000 PER year FOR 5 years, TOTALING $3,000,000 – WITHOUT recognizing ANY liability ON the BALANCE Sheet, SINCE no GOODS have YET been RECEIVED under the AGREEMENT.
8

Worked Example – a Commitment That Does Not Require Disclosure

An immaterial, routine arrangement
Example: A short-term office supplies contract
The same company also has a 6-month office supplies contract committing it to $8,000 of purchases – an immaterial amount relative to its overall $40,000,000 balance sheet.
GIVEN the GENUINELY immaterial AMOUNT relative TO the COMPANY'S overall FINANCIAL statements, NO specific disclosure is REQUIRED for THIS routine COMMITMENT under Section 3280.
