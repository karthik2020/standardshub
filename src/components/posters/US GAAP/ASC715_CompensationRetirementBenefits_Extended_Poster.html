Conceptual Framework Linkage

A benefit earned gradually, over an entire career
A defined benefit pension promise is EARNED gradually over an employee's working life, but paid out potentially decades later – creating both a genuinely long-term MEASUREMENT challenge (actuarial and discounting assumptions) and a recognition question (matching the cost to the service period that earned it). ASC 715 governs how this obligation is measured, and – critically – how the resulting VOLATILITY in that measurement flows through the financial statements.
The single biggest remaining OCI-mechanics divergence with IFRS
While ASC 715 and IAS 19 are CONVERGED on core actuarial measurement technique (Section 4) and full funded-status balance sheet recognition (Section 6), they diverge SHARPLY on how actuarial gains and losses subsequently flow through profit or loss versus OCI (Sections 8–9) – arguably the single most consequential, most heavily tested employee benefits divergence in the entire US GAAP/IFRS landscape.
2

Scope & Applicability

Retirement benefit plans of all kinds
ASC 715 applies to DEFINED CONTRIBUTION plans (715-70), DEFINED BENEFIT pension plans (715-20/30), other DEFINED BENEFIT postretirement plans such as retiree healthcare (715-60), and MULTIEMPLOYER plans (715-80) – covering essentially the full landscape of employer-sponsored retirement benefit arrangements.
3

Defined Contribution vs Defined Benefit Plans

A fundamentally different risk allocation, and accounting complexity
Under a DEFINED CONTRIBUTION plan, the employer's obligation is simply to make the specified CONTRIBUTION each period – expense equals the required contribution, with NO further actuarial complexity, since investment and longevity risk rest with the EMPLOYEE. Under a DEFINED BENEFIT plan, the employer promises a specified future BENEFIT (e.g. a percentage of final salary per year of service) – bearing the investment and longevity risk itself, requiring the FULL actuarial measurement apparatus this Topic is primarily concerned with.
4

Measuring the Defined Benefit Obligation – the Projected Unit Credit Method

Fully converged actuarial technique
The defined benefit obligation – called the PROJECTED BENEFIT OBLIGATION (PBO) under US GAAP – is measured using the PROJECTED UNIT CREDIT METHOD, an actuarial technique attributing benefit to periods of service (including projected future salary increases) and discounting the resulting obligation to present value. This is the IDENTICAL core actuarial method IAS 19 requires – one of the most precisely converged specific techniques anywhere in this library.
5

Plan Assets at Fair Value

Also converged
Plan assets set aside to fund the benefit obligation are measured at FAIR VALUE at each measurement date – the same measurement basis IAS 19 requires for plan assets.
6

Funded Status on the Balance Sheet

Full recognition – no more deferral, on either side
The NET FUNDED STATUS – PBO minus the fair value of plan assets – is recognised in FULL on the Balance Sheet, as either a net asset (overfunded) or net liability (underfunded). This is fully converged with IAS 19's OWN post-2011 approach, which similarly eliminated its historical "corridor approach" for BALANCE SHEET recognition purposes, requiring the full net defined benefit liability/asset to be recognised – both frameworks now present the SAME full funded status figure on the balance sheet. The divergence that remains lives entirely in HOW that funded status change flows through the INCOME STATEMENT versus OCI (Sections 8–9).

8

The Corridor Approach – Smoothing Actuarial Gains/Losses Into Net Income

The headline, heavily tested US GAAP mechanic
Actuarial GAINS and LOSSES (arising from changes in assumptions or differences between expected and actual experience) are initially recognised in OCI – but UNLIKE most other OCI items in this library, they are then SUBSEQUENTLY AMORTISED into NET PERIODIC PENSION COST over the employees' AVERAGE REMAINING SERVICE PERIOD (or remaining life expectancy, for a plan with no active employees) – but ONLY to the extent the CUMULATIVE unrecognised gain/loss EXCEEDS a "CORRIDOR" threshold, defined as 10% of the GREATER of the PBO or the fair value of plan assets. Amounts WITHIN the 10% corridor are never amortised at all; only the EXCESS above the corridor gets spread into net income over time. This is a genuine, ongoing SMOOTHING mechanism – actuarial volatility is deliberately spread out and only partially, gradually recognised in profit or loss.
9

IAS 19's Contrasting "No Recycling" Remeasurement Model

Full, immediate recognition with permanent OCI treatment
IAS 19 (as revised in 2011) ELIMINATED any corridor or smoothing mechanism entirely. ACTUARIAL GAINS/LOSSES (termed "REMEASUREMENTS" under IAS 19) are recognised IMMEDIATELY AND FULLY in OCI in the period they arise – and are NEVER SUBSEQUENTLY RECLASSIFIED (recycled) to profit or loss, under any circumstances, for the life of the plan. This is functionally similar in spirit to the "permanent OCI, no recycling" treatment IFRS 9 allows for the equity FVOCI election (discussed in the ASC 220/321 posters) – a recurring IFRS pattern of keeping certain volatile remeasurements permanently out of profit or loss, rather than smoothing them into it gradually the way US GAAP's corridor approach does.
10

Expected Return on Plan Assets vs IAS 19's Net Interest Approach

A related, second smoothing-vs-no-smoothing divergence
US GAAP separately calculates an EXPECTED LONG-TERM RATE OF RETURN on plan assets for net periodic cost purposes – a SMOOTHED, assumed return, DISTINCT from the plan's ACTUAL return for the period. Any DIFFERENCE between the expected and actual return becomes part of the actuarial gain/loss subject to the corridor amortisation in Section 8. IAS 19 (post-2011) does NOT use an expected-return concept at all for net periodic cost – instead, it applies the SAME discount rate used for the defined benefit obligation to the plan assets, computing a single "NET INTEREST" figure, with ANY difference between the plan's ACTUAL return and this net interest amount treated as a REMEASUREMENT, recognised immediately (and permanently) in OCI with no smoothing whatsoever – the same "no recycling" philosophy from Section 9, applied here to the asset-return component specifically.
11

Multiemployer Plans (715-80)

Contribution-based accounting, with enhanced disclosure
Participation in a MULTIEMPLOYER defined benefit plan (common in unionised industries, pooling multiple unrelated employers' contributions) is generally accounted for SIMILARLY to a defined contribution plan for RECOGNITION purposes – expense equals required contributions – but with SPECIFIC ADDITIONAL disclosures given the well-known "WITHDRAWAL LIABILITY" risk unique to these arrangements (a participating employer can become liable for a share of the plan's UNDERFUNDING if it withdraws, or if OTHER participating employers withdraw or become insolvent). IAS 19 addresses multiemployer plans similarly (contribution-based accounting when sufficient information for full defined benefit accounting isn't available), though with somewhat different specific disclosure detail.

Worked Example – Net Periodic Pension Cost With Corridor Amortisation

Assembling the components, with a corridor-triggered amortisation
Example: A plan with a cumulative unrecognised loss exceeding the corridor
Service cost $800,000; interest cost $600,000; expected return on plan assets $(450,000); cumulative unrecognised actuarial loss $3,200,000; PBO $10,000,000; fair value of plan assets $8,000,000; average remaining service period of active employees, 10 years.
Corridor = 10% × $10,000,000 (the greater of PBO/plan assets) = $1,000,000. Amount EXCEEDING the corridor = $3,200,000 − $1,000,000 = $2,200,000. Annual amortisation = $2,200,000 ÷ 10 years = $220,000.
Net periodic pension cost = $800,000 (service) + $600,000 (interest) − $450,000 (expected return) + $220,000 (amortisation) = $1,170,000.

Worked Example – Contrasting OCI Treatment (US vs IFRS)

Same actuarial loss, two very different eventual outcomes
Example: A $2,200,000 actuarial loss arising in the current year
A plan experiences a $2,200,000 actuarial loss during the year due to a change in discount rate assumptions.
Under US GAAP: the full $2,200,000 is recognised in OCI initially, but will be GRADUALLY AMORTISED into net periodic pension cost (and thus INTO net income) over future years, to the extent it exceeds the corridor – EVENTUALLY flowing through profit or loss.
Under IAS 19: the full $2,200,000 remeasurement is recognised in OCI and STAYS there PERMANENTLY – it will NEVER flow through profit or loss, in any future period, under any circumstances. The SAME actuarial event produces a temporary OCI item under US GAAP that eventually reaches net income, versus a permanent OCI item under IFRS that never does.

Quick-reference glossary preview
Corridor
The threshold – 10% of the greater of the PBO or plan asset fair value – beyond which cumulative unrecognised actuarial gains/losses must be amortised into net periodic pension cost.
Projected benefit obligation (PBO)
The actuarial present value of benefits attributed to employee service, including projected future salary increases, measured using the projected unit credit method.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Net periodic pension cost
The total pension expense for a period, combining service cost, interest cost, expected return on plan assets, and amortisation of prior service cost and actuarial gains/losses.
Remeasurement (IAS 19 term)
IAS 19's term for actuarial gains/losses and the difference between actual and expected plan asset returns, recognised immediately and permanently in OCI.
Withdrawal liability
The potential liability a participating employer in a multiemployer plan may incur for a share of the plan's underfunding upon withdrawal.
