1

Conceptual Framework Linkage

One consistent definition of "fair value," used everywhere it's required
Dozens of Topics throughout the Codification require fair value measurement (ASC 320's AFS securities, ASC 321's equity securities, ASC 805's business combination assets, ASC 815's derivatives, and many more) — but WITHOUT a single, consistent DEFINITION and MEASUREMENT FRAMEWORK, "fair value" could mean something different every time. ASC 820 supplies that single, consistent framework, applied WHEREVER another Topic calls for fair value.
One of the most thoroughly converged standards in the entire library
ASC 820 and IFRS 13 emerged from a genuine JOINT FASB-IASB project, finalised in 2011 SPECIFICALLY to converge fair value measurement guidance — making this, alongside ASC 606/IFRS 15, one of the clearest examples of TRUE standard-level convergence anywhere in the Codification.
2

Scope — a "How to Measure," Not "When to Measure," Topic

A measurement framework, not a recognition trigger
ASC 820 does NOT itself determine WHEN fair value measurement is required — that decision is made by whichever OTHER Topic governs the specific asset, liability, or transaction (e.g. ASC 321 requires fair value for equity securities; ASC 820 then supplies HOW to actually measure that fair value once required).
3

The Exit Price Notion

What a market participant would pay or receive, not what the entity paid
Fair value is defined as the price that would be RECEIVED to SELL an asset, or PAID to TRANSFER a liability, in an ORDERLY transaction between MARKET PARTICIPANTS at the measurement date — an EXIT PRICE, not an entry (acquisition) price, and not necessarily the price the reporting entity itself paid or would pay. This exit-price orientation is identical to IFRS 13's own definition.
4

Principal vs Most Advantageous Market

Converged
Fair value is measured based on the PRINCIPAL market for the asset/liability (the market with the greatest volume/activity), or, in the absence of a principal market, the MOST ADVANTAGEOUS market (maximising the amount received or minimising the amount paid, after transaction costs) — the identical hierarchy IFRS 13 applies.
5

The Fair Value Hierarchy — Level 1, 2, 3

An identical three-level hierarchy under both frameworks
LevelDescription
Level 1Quoted prices in ACTIVE markets for IDENTICAL assets/liabilities — the most reliable, least judgemental input
Level 2Observable inputs OTHER THAN Level 1 quoted prices — e.g. quoted prices for similar assets, or inputs derived from observable market data
Level 3UNOBSERVABLE inputs — the entity's own assumptions, used only when observable inputs are unavailable
This is precisely the SAME three-level hierarchy structure IFRS 13 uses — one of the clearest, most literally converged mechanics in this entire library.
6

Valuation Techniques

Three approaches, consistently applied under both frameworks
Fair value may be estimated using the MARKET approach (prices from actual market transactions for identical/comparable items), the INCOME approach (discounting future cash flows or earnings to present value), or the COST approach (current replacement cost) — the same three valuation technique categories IFRS 13 recognises, applied consistently, with the choice depending on which technique is most appropriate given available data.
7

Highest and Best Use for Nonfinancial Assets

Converged concept
For NONFINANCIAL assets, fair value measurement assumes the asset's HIGHEST AND BEST USE by market participants — which may DIFFER from the asset's CURRENT use by the reporting entity — physically possible, legally permissible, and financially feasible. This is the same highest-and-best-use principle IFRS 13 applies.
8

Worked Example — Level 1 vs Level 2 vs Level 3 Classification

Three instruments, three different hierarchy levels
Example: A portfolio of investments
An entity holds: (a) publicly traded shares on a major stock exchange, (b) a corporate bond with no active quoted market but whose value is estimated using observable yield curves and credit spreads for similar bonds, and (c) a private equity fund interest valued using unobservable projected cash flows and management's own discount rate assumptions.
(a) is Level 1 — a quoted price in an active market for an identical asset. (b) is Level 2 — valued using observable market inputs (yield curves, credit spreads), even without a direct quoted price. (c) is Level 3 — valued using genuinely unobservable, entity-specific assumptions, warranting the MOST extensive disclosure of the three.
9

Worked Example — Highest and Best Use

Valuing land at its optimal use, not its current use
Example: Land currently used for parking, zoned for mixed-use development
An entity owns land currently used as a surface parking lot, but the land is zoned for, and market participants would realistically develop it into, a mixed-use residential/retail building.
Fair value is measured based on the land's HIGHEST AND BEST USE (mixed-use development potential), NOT its current parking-lot use — even though the entity itself has no current plans to develop the site, since market participants generally WOULD pursue that higher-value use.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Market participants
Buyers and sellers in the principal (or most advantageous) market who are independent, knowledgeable, and willing and able to transact.
Highest and best use
The use of a nonfinancial asset by market participants that maximises its value, which may differ from the entity's current use of the asset.
