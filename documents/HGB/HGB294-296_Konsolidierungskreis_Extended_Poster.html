Conceptual Framework Linkage

From "who controls whom" to "who actually goes into the numbers"
Where §290 established WHO must prepare a Konzernabschluss, and identified WHICH relationships constitute control, §§294–296 answer the NEXT question: of every subsidiary a parent controls, which ones must ACTUALLY be consolidated, and are any genuinely excludable?
HOW §§294–296 MAP TO GERMAN ACCOUNTING PRINCIPLES
Vollständigkeitsgebot, at group level — §294(1)'s default is INCLUSION of every controlled subsidiary, mirroring the same completeness presumption §246(1) established for individual assets and liabilities Proportionality, narrowly applied — §296's exclusion grounds are genuinely NARROW exceptions to that default, not a broad opt-out
What §§294–296 require, at a glance
§Core requirement

295Repealed — formerly a dissimilar-activities exclusion, abolished in 2004
296Four narrow exclusion grounds, with an anti-abuse aggregation rule
2

Scope

Every subsidiary caught by the §290 control test
§§294–296 apply to every subsidiary a parent has been determined to control under the §290(2) tests established in the previous poster — the DEFAULT rule is inclusion; exclusion is available ONLY through the specific, narrow §296 grounds.
3

§294(1) — Full Inclusion, Regardless of Registered Seat

Foreign subsidiaries are captured just as fully as domestic ones
§294(1) requires ALL subsidiaries of the parent to be included in the Konzernabschluss — REGARDLESS of their registered seat, meaning FOREIGN subsidiaries (wherever located) are captured exactly as fully as German ones, UNLESS a specific §296 exclusion applies. There is no general "foreign subsidiary" carve-out; only the narrow, named grounds in §296 (Section 7) can justify exclusion.
4

§294(3) — the Parent's Legal Right to Information

A genuine, legally enforceable right — not dependent on voluntary cooperation
§294(3) grants the parent a genuine LEGAL RIGHT to demand from its subsidiaries — and from THEIR OWN subsidiaries, reaching down through multiple tiers — all documents and information necessary for preparing the Konzernabschluss. This ensures the parent's consolidation obligation is never practically frustrated by an uncooperative subsidiary's own management, since the parent can compel production rather than merely request it.

Worked Example — Compelling a Reluctant Subsidiary to Cooperate

A legal entitlement, reaching down through multiple tiers
Example 1: A sub-subsidiary's local management resisting disclosure
A German parent's second-tier foreign sub-subsidiary — itself owned by a first-tier intermediate subsidiary — has local management reluctant to share detailed financial records, citing local confidentiality concerns.
§294(3) gives the German parent a direct LEGAL RIGHT to demand the necessary information from this sub-subsidiary DESPITE it sitting two tiers down the ownership chain — local management's reluctance does not defeat this statutory right, and the parent may compel production through the intermediate subsidiary if necessary.
6

§295 — Repealed, With a Genuinely Interesting History

A former "dissimilar activities" prohibition, abolished in 2004
§295 formerly implemented a consolidation PROHIBITION rooted in the old EU Group Accounts Directive's Article 14 — permitting (or in some readings, requiring) exclusion of a subsidiary whose activities were so DIFFERENT from the rest of the group that including it would be incompatible with giving a true and fair view (the classic example being a manufacturing group with one financial-services subsidiary). This provision was ABOLISHED by the 2004 IFRS-Bilanzrechtsreformgesetz, reflecting a broader international shift AWAY from dissimilar-activities exclusions — the SAME shift IFRS itself made when IAS 27/IFRS 10 eliminated their own equivalent exclusion, on the reasoning that dissimilar activities are better addressed through segment reporting than through excluding an entity from consolidation entirely.
7

§296(1) — the Four Grounds for Excluding a Controlled Subsidiary

Narrow, specific, and optional — never automatic
§296(1) permits (NEVER requires) a controlled subsidiary to be excluded from consolidation on any of the following grounds:
Severe and lasting restrictions substantially impairing the parent's ability to exercise its rights over the subsidiary's assets or management (e.g. exchange controls, expropriation risk, war) Disproportionate cost or delay in obtaining the information necessary for consolidation Shares held exclusively with a view to subsequent resale (a genuinely temporary holding) Subordinate importance (immateriality) to giving a true and fair view of the group's position
8

Worked Example — Severe and Lasting Restrictions

Political and currency risk genuinely preventing the exercise of control
Example 2: A subsidiary trapped by severe capital controls
A German parent's subsidiary operates in a country that has imposed severe, sustained capital controls and expropriation risk following a political crisis, genuinely preventing the parent from repatriating funds, exercising voting control, or directing the subsidiary's management in any meaningful way.
The parent MAY exclude this subsidiary from consolidation under §296(1)'s first ground — the restrictions are both SEVERE and LASTING, substantially impairing the parent's actual ability to exercise its otherwise-existing control rights, DESPITE technical majority ownership remaining intact on paper.
9

Worked Example — Disproportionate Cost or Delay

A genuinely high bar, not a convenience exemption
Example 3: A recently-acquired subsidiary with incompatible legacy systems
A parent acquires a small foreign subsidiary mid-year, whose legacy accounting systems are genuinely incompatible with the group's reporting requirements, such that obtaining consolidation-ready information within the reporting timeframe would require costs genuinely disproportionate to the subsidiary's own significance.
PROVIDED the cost/delay is genuinely disproportionate — not merely inconvenient — the parent MAY rely on this ground for the current period, while working to integrate the subsidiary's systems for future consolidation. This ground is applied narrowly in practice; ordinary administrative friction from a new acquisition would generally NOT meet this bar on its own.
10

Worked Example — Shares Held Exclusively for Resale

A genuinely temporary holding, illustrated by a bank rescue scenario
Example 4: A bank's temporary rescue-driven shareholding
A credit institution temporarily acquires a controlling shareholding in another troubled credit institution PURELY as part of a financial rescue/restructuring operation, with a clear, genuine intention to resell the shares once the rescued institution stabilises.
The acquiring bank MAY exclude the rescued institution from consolidation under §296(1)'s "held exclusively for resale" ground — a specifically recognised scenario in German banking-sector practice, though the bank must genuinely intend resale, not merely characterise an otherwise long-term holding as temporary to avoid consolidation.
11

Worked Example — Immateriality and the Aggregation Anti-Abuse Rule

Several small exclusions cannot be used to hide a materially significant combined effect
Example 5: Five individually immaterial subsidiaries
A parent has five small subsidiaries, each individually immaterial to the group's overall picture, but whose COMBINED balance sheet totals and revenue would represent a materially significant portion of the group if considered together.
Even though EACH subsidiary individually qualifies for the immateriality exclusion ground, §296's aggregation rule REQUIRES all five to be included ANYWAY, since TOGETHER they are not of subordinate importance — the parent cannot use several small, individually-immaterial exclusions to achieve what would effectively be a materially significant omission from the group accounts.
12

§296(3) — Justifying an Exclusion in the Konzernanhang

Never a silent omission
§296(3) requires ANY reliance on the §296(1)/(2) exclusion grounds to be JUSTIFIED and explained in the Konzernanhang (the consolidated notes, covered in a later poster) — a controlled subsidiary can never simply disappear from the group accounts without an explicit, disclosed reason readers can evaluate for themselves.

Critical Judgments & Practical Difficulties

Where judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Whether restrictions are genuinely "severe and lasting," rather than temporary or manageable, under the first §296(1) ground Whether a stated resale intention under the third ground is genuine, versus a re-characterisation of an effectively long-term holding
PRACTICAL DIFFICULTIES
Testing the aggregation rule across a large population of small subsidiaries, particularly where individual immateriality assessments were made independently by different local teams Enforcing the §294(3) information right in practice against a genuinely uncooperative multi-tier sub-subsidiary in a jurisdiction with weak legal enforcement

Relationship to IFRS (IFRS 10) — a Genuine, Significant Divergence

Comparison — HGB §296 vs IFRS 10
FeatureHGB (§296)IFRS 10
Exclusion for severe restrictionsPERMITTED (§296(1))NOT a general exclusion ground — IFRS 10 requires consolidation regardless in most such cases
Exclusion for disproportionate costPERMITTED (§296(1))NOT available — cost/effort is not a recognised IFRS 10 exclusion ground
Held-for-resale exclusionPERMITTED (§296(1))Addressed narrowly through IFRS 5 (held-for-sale classification within consolidated figures), NOT full exclusion from consolidation itself
Dissimilar activities exclusionABOLISHED in 2004 (former §295)ABOLISHED — CONVERGED
⚠️ This is a genuinely significant, easily-missed divergence: while both frameworks abolished the OLD dissimilar-activities exclusion together, HGB's §296 STILL retains three considerably BROADER exclusion grounds (severe restrictions, disproportionate cost, held-for-resale) that IFRS 10's own, considerably STRICTER consolidation model generally does not offer nearly as liberally — a controlled subsidiary that HGB would permit a parent to exclude may well still require full IFRS 10 consolidation for a dual-reporting group.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Vorlage- und Auskunftspflicht
Production and disclosure duty — the parent's legal right under §294(3) to demand consolidation-relevant information from subsidiaries.
Konsolidierungskreis
Consolidation scope — the set of subsidiaries actually included in the Konzernabschluss.
Von untergeordneter Bedeutung
Of subordinate importance — the immateriality ground for exclusion under §296(1), subject to the aggregation anti-abuse rule.
Vorübergehender Besitz
Temporary holding — the basis for the held-for-resale exclusion ground, notably relevant to bank rescue scenarios.
Konzernanhang
Consolidated notes, where any §296 exclusion must be justified per §296(3).
Konzernbilanzrichtlinie
The old EU Group Accounts Directive whose Article 14 underpinned the now-repealed §295 dissimilar-activities exclusion.
