Conceptual Framework Linkage

Cost-benefit as an organising principle
Full IFRS is designed for entities with public accountability, where the volume and complexity of disclosure serves capital market participants with diverse, sophisticated information needs. For SMEs, whose users typically have DIRECT ACCESS to management and narrower information needs, the FULL cost-benefit balance shifts – the IFRS for SMEs Standard deliberately simplifies recognition, measurement, and disclosure to reduce preparation burden without abandoning the underlying qualitative characteristics of useful financial information.
A genuinely standalone Standard, not a "lite" cross-reference
The IFRS for SMEs Standard is a SELF-CONTAINED document – an entity applying it does not need to refer back to full IFRS Standards for its day-to-day accounting. It is organised in 35 numbered sections (a fundamentally different structure from the individually numbered IAS/IFRS Standards), each addressing a specific topic area.
2

Scope & SME Definition

Defined by absence of public accountability, not by size
An entity is an SME, for purposes of this Standard, if it does NOT have public accountability and it PUBLISHES general purpose financial statements for external users. Public accountability exists if the entity's debt or equity instruments are traded in a public market (or it is in the process of issuing such instruments), OR it holds assets in a fiduciary capacity for a broad group of outsiders as one of its primary businesses (e.g. banks, insurers, brokers).
Notably, the Standard does NOT set quantified size thresholds (revenue, assets, employees) itself – individual jurisdictions adopting the Standard often layer their own size criteria on top when incorporating it into local law.
Subsidiaries of listed groups
A subsidiary whose PARENT uses full IFRS Standards in its consolidated financial statements is not thereby prevented from using the IFRS for SMEs Standard in its OWN financial statements, provided the subsidiary itself does not have public accountability.

Key Simplification Philosophy

Where the Standard consistently diverges from full IFRS
Fewer available accounting policy CHOICES than full IFRS (e.g. only the cost model for investment property where fair value cannot be measured without undue cost/effort, versus full IFRS's genuine cost/fair-value choice)Amortisation of goodwill and other intangibles with indefinite useful life, rather than the annual impairment-only model used in full IFRS (see Section 6)Simplified financial instrument classification (Sections 11–12), avoiding much of IFRS 9's complexityReduced disclosure volume throughout (see Section 10)Topics NOT addressed at all where irrelevant to typical SMEs (e.g. earnings per share, interim financial reporting, segment reporting are omitted, since SMEs' users rarely need them)
5

Financial Statement Presentation

A familiar set of statements, presented more simply
A complete set of financial statements comprises a STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION (SOFP), a STATEMENT OF PROFIT OR LOSS (SOPL) – with an option to present a single combined statement of comprehensive income where OCI items exist – a STATEMENT OF CHANGES IN EQUITY (which can be combined with SOPL in limited circumstances, i.e. when the only changes to equity arise from profit/loss, dividends, and prior period corrections), a STATEMENT OF CASH FLOWS, and NOTES. The structure closely parallels IAS 1's requirements but with a smaller mandatory disclosure set.
6

Goodwill & Intangibles – Mandatory Amortisation

The single most-cited divergence from full IFRS
Unlike full IFRS (IAS 36/IFRS 3), where goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles are NOT amortised but tested for impairment annually, the IFRS for SMEs Standard requires ALL intangible assets, including goodwill, to be AMORTISED over their useful lives. If an entity is unable to make a reliable estimate of the useful life, the life is presumed NOT to exceed 10 YEARS. This avoids the cost and judgement burden of annual impairment testing, at the cost of a less risk-sensitive measurement approach.
7

Financial Instruments – Sections 11/12

A two-tier approach avoiding IFRS 9's full complexity
Section 11 (Basic Financial Instruments) covers straightforward instruments – most trade receivables/payables, bank loans, and investments in non-convertible, non-puttable ordinary/preference shares – generally measured at AMORTISED COST (or cost less impairment for unquoted equity instruments where fair value cannot be reliably measured). Section 12 (Other Financial Instrument Issues) covers more complex instruments (derivatives, hedge accounting, more complex debt/equity instruments), generally measured at FAIR VALUE through profit or loss. This structure avoids IFRS 9's full expected-credit-loss impairment model and complex classification categories.
8

PP&E, Investment Property & Borrowing Costs

PP&E and investment property
PP&E follows the cost model, largely consistent with IAS 16, including a simplified approach to component depreciation. Investment property is measured at FAIR VALUE THROUGH PROFIT OR LOSS if fair value can be measured reliably without undue cost or effort – otherwise, the entity falls back to the PP&E cost model. This differs from full IFRS's IAS 40, which offers a genuine free choice between the cost and fair value models regardless of measurement difficulty.
Borrowing costs
Unlike full IFRS (IAS 23), which REQUIRES capitalisation of borrowing costs directly attributable to qualifying assets, the IFRS for SMEs Standard requires ALL borrowing costs to be EXPENSED as incurred – a deliberate simplification removing an area of judgement (identifying qualifying assets, capitalisation rates) that full IFRS entities must navigate.
9

Income Tax & Business Combinations

Broadly aligned, with narrower guidance
Income tax (Section 29) follows a temporary-difference deferred tax approach broadly consistent with IAS 12's core mechanics, though with less detailed guidance on complex scenarios. Business combinations (Section 19) require the PURCHASE (acquisition) method, consistent with IFRS 3, but with simpler guidance on areas such as contingent consideration and non-controlling interests, and – as noted in Section 6 – resulting goodwill is amortised rather than tested only for impairment.
10

Disclosure – Reduced Volume

A deliberately lighter disclosure footprint
Disclosure requirements throughout the Standard are substantially REDUCED relative to full IFRS – reflecting that SME users typically have more direct access to supplementary information from management, reducing their reliance on extensive note disclosure. Areas with the most significant reduction include financial instruments (a fraction of IFRS 7's extensive risk disclosures), employee benefits, and share-based payment.
11

Worked Example – Goodwill Amortisation

Contrasting the SME and full IFRS outcomes
Example: Goodwill following an acquisition, useful life not reliably estimable
An SME acquires a business, recognising goodwill of $900,000. Management cannot make a reliable estimate of goodwill's useful life.
Under the IFRS for SMEs Standard, the useful life is PRESUMED not to exceed 10 years – the SME amortises $90,000 per year on a straight-line basis (subject to impairment testing only where indicators exist), producing a steadily declining goodwill balance. Under FULL IFRS (IFRS 3/IAS 36), the same $900,000 would instead remain UNAMORTISED and be tested for impairment annually regardless of indicators – potentially remaining at $900,000 indefinitely if no impairment is triggered, a materially different balance sheet trajectory for the identical acquisition.
12

Transition to the IFRS for SMEs Standard

A dedicated transition section (Section 35)
An entity adopting the IFRS for SMEs Standard for the first time applies Section 35, which – much like IFRS 1 for full IFRS – generally requires FULL RETROSPECTIVE application, subject to specified mandatory exceptions and optional exemptions designed to ease the transition (for example, relief on retrospectively remeasuring past business combinations).

IFRS for SMEs vs Full IFRS vs US GAAP (Private Company Council)

GoodwillAmortised (≤10yr presumption)Impairment-only, never amortisedUS private companies MAY elect to amortise goodwill over 10 years via a Private Company Council (PCC) alternative – a similar simplification philosophy to IFRS for SMEs
Borrowing costsAlways expensedCapitalised for qualifying assetsCapitalisation generally required, similar to full IFRS, with no broad SME-style expensing option

The PCC's targeted alternatives (goodwill amortisation, simpler hedge accounting, etc.) represent a philosophically similar but STRUCTURALLY different approach: the US opted to bolt private-company relief onto its single GAAP codification, rather than issue a fully separate, self-contained standard as the IASB did.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Public accountability
The defining scope test for the IFRS for SMEs Standard – an entity has public accountability if its debt/equity trades in a public market, or it holds assets in a fiduciary capacity for a broad group of outsiders as a primary business.
Undue cost or effort
A recurring threshold throughout the Standard (e.g. for investment property fair value measurement) permitting a fallback to a simpler treatment where the cost of applying a requirement would clearly outweigh the benefit to users.
Section (vs Standard)
The IFRS for SMEs Standard's internal organisational unit – 35 numbered Sections within one document, contrasted with the many separately numbered IAS/IFRS Standards that make up full IFRS.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position SOPL Statement of profit or loss OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
