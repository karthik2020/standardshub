1

Conceptual Framework Linkage

Recognising revenue as work progresses, rather than only at completion
ASBJ Statement No. 15 addresses REVENUE and cost recognition for CONSTRUCTION-type contracts – arrangements typically spanning MULTIPLE accounting periods, where the CENTRAL question is WHETHER revenue should be recognised PROGRESSIVELY, as work is performed (the PERCENTAGE-OF-COMPLETION method), or only ONCE the contract is substantially COMPLETE (the COMPLETED-CONTRACT method).
2

Scope

Construction and other long-term contract arrangements
This Statement applies to CONSTRUCTION contracts and OTHER long-term contract arrangements (e.g. certain custom manufacturing or software development contracts) SPANNING more than a SINGLE accounting period.
3

The Percentage-of-Completion Method

Recognising revenue in proportion to progress, most commonly via the cost-to-cost method
Under the PERCENTAGE-OF-COMPLETION method, REVENUE and COSTS are recognised in PROPORTION to the contract's progress toward completion – most commonly measured using the "COST-TO-COST" method (costs incurred TO DATE divided by TOTAL estimated contract costs) – PROVIDED the OUTCOME of the contract can be estimated RELIABLY (total contract revenue, costs, and stage of completion can all be measured with reasonable CONFIDENCE).
4

The Completed-Contract Method

Deferring all revenue recognition until substantial completion
Where the OUTCOME of a contract CANNOT be reliably estimated, the COMPLETED-CONTRACT method applies instead – DEFERRING all revenue recognition until the contract is SUBSTANTIALLY complete, at which point the ENTIRE contract revenue and related costs are recognised TOGETHER.
5

Choosing Between the Two Methods

Reliability of estimation is the deciding factor
The CHOICE between the two methods is NOT a free ACCOUNTING POLICY election – it depends DIRECTLY on whether the contract's outcome can be estimated RELIABLY. Where reliable estimation IS possible, the percentage-of-completion method is generally PREFERRED, as it provides a more FAITHFUL, TIMELY representation of the entity's ECONOMIC performance as work progresses.
6

Anticipated Losses on Construction Contracts

The entire expected loss recognised immediately
Where TOTAL estimated contract costs EXCEED total estimated contract revenue (an ANTICIPATED LOSS), the ENTIRE expected loss is recognised IMMEDIATELY, in the period the loss becomes EVIDENT – regardless of the contract's CURRENT stage of completion – reflecting the PRUDENT principle that a KNOWN future loss should not be deferred simply because the underlying WORK has not yet finished.
7

Absorption Into the General Revenue Recognition Model (ASBJ Statement No. 29)

Construction contracts now analysed under the general over-time/point-in-time framework
Since ASBJ Statement No. 29, Accounting Standard for Revenue Recognition, came into effect, construction contract revenue is now GENERALLY analysed under the GENERAL revenue recognition model – determining whether a performance obligation is satisfied OVER TIME or at a POINT IN TIME, based on criteria broadly SIMILAR to IFRS 15's own framework – rather than under THIS Statement's separately-named percentage-of-completion/completed-contract terminology. In PRACTICE, the SUBSTANTIVE OUTCOME for most construction contracts is broadly SIMILAR under both frameworks (progressive revenue recognition where the customer receives and consumes benefits as work progresses), but the ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK and terminology used has shifted to the GENERAL revenue standard, PARALLELING how IAS 11 (Construction Contracts) was INTERNATIONALLY WITHDRAWN and folded into IFRS 15 when THAT standard was introduced.
8

Worked Example – Percentage-of-Completion Using the Cost-to-Cost Method

Recognising revenue in proportion to costs incurred
Example: A ¥500,000,000 building construction contract
A construction company has a contract with total expected revenue of ¥500,000,000 and total estimated costs of ¥400,000,000. By the end of the current period, ¥160,000,000 of costs have been incurred.
Percentage of completion = ¥160,000,000 ÷ ¥400,000,000 = 40%. Revenue recognised for the period = 40% × ¥500,000,000 = ¥200,000,000, with a MATCHING ¥160,000,000 of costs recognised, resulting in ¥40,000,000 of gross profit recognised for the period.
9

Worked Example – Recognising an Anticipated Loss

Immediate recognition of the full expected shortfall
Example: Cost overruns turning a profitable contract into a loss
Continuing the prior example, cost overruns mean total estimated contract costs are REVISED UPWARD to ¥540,000,000, against the SAME ¥500,000,000 total contract revenue – an anticipated LOSS of ¥40,000,000 overall.
The company recognises the ENTIRE ¥40,000,000 anticipated loss IMMEDIATELY in the CURRENT period (in addition to any loss already reflected through normal percentage-of-completion accounting to date) – the FULL expected shortfall is NOT deferred across the remaining periods of the contract.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Percentage-of-completion method
Recognising contract revenue and costs in proportion to progress toward completion, most commonly via the cost-to-cost method.
Completed-contract method
Deferring all revenue recognition until a contract is substantially complete, used when the outcome cannot be reliably estimated.
Anticipated loss
The full amount by which total estimated contract costs exceed total estimated contract revenue, recognised immediately in the period it becomes evident.
