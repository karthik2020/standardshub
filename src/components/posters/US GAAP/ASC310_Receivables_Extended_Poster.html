Conceptual Framework Linkage

A right to future cash, carrying genuine collection risk
A receivable is a contractual right to receive cash or another financial asset – meeting the Conceptual Framework's asset definition, but carrying real UNCERTAINTY about full collection. ASC 310 governs how receivables are initially recognised, and works alongside ASC 326 (credit losses) to ensure the CARRYING AMOUNT faithfully reflects the amount genuinely expected to be collected, not simply the contractual face amount.
One of the most consequential post-financial-crisis reforms
The credit loss measurement model governing receivables was FUNDAMENTALLY overhauled by ASU 2016-13, introducing the CURRENT EXPECTED CREDIT LOSS (CECL) model – a direct regulatory response to concerns that the pre-2016 "incurred loss" model recognised credit losses too LATE, only after a loss event had already occurred. This makes the credit loss estimation methodology (Section 5, elaborated fully in the ASC 326 poster) one of the most significant differences between US GAAP and IFRS in the entire Codification.
2

Scope & Applicability

Trade and financing receivables
ASC 310 applies to receivables generally – including TRADE receivables (from ordinary sale of goods/services) and FINANCING receivables (loans, notes receivable, and similar financing arrangements) – for all entities, though its practical significance is greatest for financial institutions and entities extending significant trade credit.
3

Initial Recognition & Measurement

Face amount, adjusted for the time value of money where significant
Receivables are initially recognised at their FACE (contractual) amount – for most trade receivables with short settlement terms, no discounting is required. For LONGER-term receivables where a significant financing component exists, the receivable is measured at the PRESENT VALUE of expected future cash flows, broadly consistent with how IFRS 15 requires an entity to adjust the transaction price for a significant financing component.
4

Nonrefundable Fees and Costs (310-20)

Deferred and recognised as a yield adjustment
NONREFUNDABLE loan origination FEES received, and direct loan origination COSTS incurred, are DEFERRED and recognised over the life of the related loan as an adjustment to the loan's effective YIELD (using the effective interest method) – NOT recognised immediately in income. This produces a result functionally similar to how IFRS 9 incorporates transaction costs and fees directly into the EFFECTIVE INTEREST RATE calculation for financial assets measured at amortised cost, even though the two frameworks arrive at this outcome through separately drafted guidance.
5

Allowance for Credit Losses – Cross-Reference to CECL (ASC 326)

Detailed CECL mechanics live in ASC 326, not here
Since ASU 2016-13, the DETAILED measurement of expected credit losses on receivables is governed by ASC 326 (Financial Instruments–Credit Losses) rather than ASC 310 itself – ASC 310 retains general receivable recognition/classification guidance, while ASC 326 supplies the CECL methodology. The headline conceptual point: CECL requires recognising a LIFETIME expected credit loss allowance at ORIGINATION or ACQUISITION of a receivable, regardless of whether credit quality has since deteriorated – a fundamentally more forward-looking, "day-one" full-lifetime-loss approach than the staged model IFRS 9 uses (elaborated fully in Section 12).
6

Purchased Financial Assets with Credit Deterioration (PCD)

A specific category for acquired, already-deteriorated assets
Loans and debt securities ACQUIRED with evidence of MORE-THAN-INSIGNIFICANT credit deterioration since origination – "PCD" assets – receive specific treatment: the initial allowance for expected credit losses is added DIRECTLY to the purchase price to establish the asset's initial amortised cost basis (rather than being recognised as a day-one expense), reflecting that the discount paid for the asset already substantially factored in the expected credit deterioration.
7

Troubled Debt Restructurings – the ASU 2022-02 Simplification

A genuinely recent simplification
Historically, ASC 310-40 provided specific recognition and measurement guidance for "troubled debt restructurings" (TDRs) – concessions granted to a borrower experiencing financial difficulty. ASU 2022-02 ELIMINATED the separate TDR recognition and measurement guidance for entities that have adopted CECL, on the basis that CECL's forward-looking, lifetime-loss allowance already captures the economics a separate TDR model previously existed to address. In its place, ASU 2022-02 introduced ENHANCED DISCLOSURE requirements specifically for loan modifications made to borrowers experiencing financial difficulty – a genuinely recent (2022) shift in US practice.

Worked Example – Nonrefundable Fee Deferral

Spreading an origination fee over the loan term
Example: A $50,000 loan origination fee on a 5-year loan
A lender originates a $2,000,000, 5-year loan and receives a $50,000 nonrefundable origination fee, while incurring $10,000 of direct origination costs.
The NET $40,000 ($50,000 fee − $10,000 costs) is DEFERRED and recognised over the 5-year loan term as an adjustment to the effective yield (typically using the effective interest method), rather than recognised immediately as fee income – increasing the loan's effective interest rate above its stated contractual rate.
9

Worked Example – Basic Receivable Recognition

Recognising a trade receivable and its allowance
Example: A trade sale on credit
An entity sells goods for $500,000 on 60-day credit terms. Applying CECL, the entity estimates a lifetime expected credit loss allowance of $8,000 based on historical loss rates, current conditions, and reasonable forecasts.
Entry: Debit Accounts receivable $500,000 / Credit Revenue $500,000 (per ASC 606). Debit Credit loss expense $8,000 / Credit Allowance for credit losses $8,000 – recognised IMMEDIATELY at origination, reflecting the full LIFETIME expected loss, consistent with CECL's day-one approach.

ASC 310/326 vs IFRS 9 – CECL vs the Three-Stage ECL Model

Comparison – CECL vs IFRS 9's expected credit loss model
FeatureUS GAAP – CECL (ASC 326)IFRS 9 – Three-Stage ECL Model
Initial (day-one) allowanceFULL LIFETIME expected credit losses, recognised immediately at origination/acquisitionOnly 12-MONTH expected credit losses at initial recognition (Stage 1) – a smaller initial allowance
Staging conceptNO staging – a single measurement basis (lifetime ECL) applies throughout the asset's lifeTHREE stages: Stage 1 (12-month ECL, performing), Stage 2 (lifetime ECL, significant credit deterioration), Stage 3 (lifetime ECL, credit-impaired)
Trigger for moving to lifetime ECLNot applicable – always lifetime from day oneA SIGNIFICANT INCREASE in credit risk since initial recognition moves an asset from Stage 1 to Stage 2
Overall day-one earnings impactLARGER immediate expense at origination (full lifetime loss upfront)SMALLER immediate expense at origination (only 12-month loss upfront), with lifetime loss recognised only later if credit quality deteriorates
⚠️ This is widely regarded as THE single largest measurement divergence between US GAAP and IFRS in financial instruments accounting today – CECL's simpler, single-basis, always-lifetime approach trades away IFRS 9's staging complexity, but produces materially LARGER day-one credit loss expense recognition for newly originated performing loans and receivables.

The post-2016 US GAAP credit loss model requiring immediate recognition of lifetime expected credit losses at origination or acquisition of a financial asset.
PCD asset
A purchased financial asset with more-than-insignificant credit deterioration since origination, receiving specific initial measurement treatment.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Troubled debt restructuring (TDR)
A concession granted by a creditor to a debtor experiencing financial difficulty – recognition/measurement guidance eliminated for CECL adopters by ASU 2022-02, replaced by enhanced disclosure.
Nonrefundable loan origination fee
A fee charged to a borrower at loan origination that the lender does not have to return, deferred and recognised as a yield adjustment over the loan's life.
