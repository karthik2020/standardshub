Conceptual Framework Linkage

Uncertainty resolved only by future events
A contingency is an existing condition, situation, or set of circumstances involving UNCERTAINTY as to a possible gain or loss, to be resolved only when one or more FUTURE events occur or fail to occur. ASC 450 governs how that uncertainty is reflected – with a deliberately ASYMMETRIC approach: losses are recognised earlier (once probable and estimable), while gains are recognised only once realised, reflecting a conservative bias against overstating assets or income.
The direct counterpart to IAS 37 – with real measurement differences
ASC 450 addresses substantially the SAME underlying problem IAS 37 governs, using a broadly similar recognition threshold – but the MEASUREMENT of a recognised loss (Section 5), and the treatment of a RANGE of possible outcomes, differ in genuinely consequential ways worth knowing well (Section 14).

Scope & Applicability

Loss and gain contingencies generally
ASC 450 applies broadly to LOSS contingencies (450-20) – potential obligations such as litigation, product warranties not otherwise addressed by specific Topics, and guarantees – and GAIN contingencies (450-30) – potential future benefits such as a pending favourable lawsuit outcome, tax refund claims, or expected insurance recoveries.
3

The Three Probability Tiers

The foundational vocabulary used throughout US GAAP contingency guidance
TierMeaning
PROBABLEThe future event(s) are LIKELY to occur – generally interpreted as a high likelihood, though not a precisely defined numeric threshold
REASONABLY POSSIBLEThe chance of the future event(s) occurring is MORE than remote but LESS than likely
REMOTEThe chance of the future event(s) occurring is SLIGHT
This is the SAME three-tier vocabulary referenced throughout this library (ASC 275's "reasonably possible" disclosure trigger, and ASC 205-40's going concern "substantial doubt" assessment both draw on this foundational language).

Loss Contingencies – Recognition Criteria

Probable AND reasonably estimable – both required
A loss contingency is ACCRUED (recognised as a liability and expense) only if BOTH: (a) it is PROBABLE that a liability has been incurred as of the balance sheet date, AND (b) the amount can be REASONABLY ESTIMATED. If probable but NOT reasonably estimable, the contingency is DISCLOSED but not accrued. If merely REASONABLY POSSIBLE (not probable), it is DISCLOSED only. If REMOTE, generally no disclosure is required (with limited exceptions, e.g. certain guarantees).
5

Measuring a Range of Loss – the Minimum-of-Range Rule

A distinctly US GAAP measurement convention
If a RANGE of possible loss exists, and NO amount within that range is a BETTER estimate than any other, ASC 450 requires accruing the MINIMUM amount in the range (with the balance of the range disclosed, if material). This differs from IAS 37, which instead applies an "EXPECTED VALUE" approach – particularly for a LARGE POPULATION of similar items, weighting all possible outcomes by their probability to arrive at a statistically expected amount, which is often closer to the MIDPOINT of a range than to its minimum. For a SINGLE, discrete obligation with a range of possible outcomes, IAS 37 instead often uses the MOST LIKELY single outcome – still a genuinely different measurement philosophy from ASC 450's minimum-of-range default.
6

Gain Contingencies – Not Recognised Until Realised

A deliberately conservative, asymmetric treatment
Gain contingencies are NOT recognised in the financial statements until REALISED or REALISABLE – even if the gain is PROBABLE – reflecting a conservative bias against anticipating income before it is essentially assured. This parallels IAS 37's own treatment of contingent assets, which similarly are not recognised until realisation is VIRTUALLY CERTAIN, and are disclosed only when an inflow of economic benefits is PROBABLE – a broadly similar asymmetric philosophy across both frameworks, even if the specific terminology ("realised/realisable" vs "virtually certain") differs somewhat.
7

Litigation Contingencies

A heavily litigated, practically significant application
Assessing pending or threatened litigation requires evaluating available evidence (including input from legal counsel), the entity's own history with similar claims, and the specific stage of proceedings – a genuinely judgement-intensive area, given the natural TENSION between an entity's legal counsel's incentive toward caution in written assessments (often for privilege reasons) and the accounting need for a genuine probability assessment.
8

Unasserted Claims

A two-stage assessment
For an UNASSERTED claim (one not yet formally brought against the entity), ASC 450 first requires assessing whether ASSERTION of the claim is itself PROBABLE. Only if assertion is deemed probable does the entity then proceed to assess the probability of an UNFAVOURABLE OUTCOME and whether the amount is reasonably estimable – a distinct, sequential two-stage evaluation specific to unasserted claims.
9

Insurance Recoveries

A gain contingency, generally not netted against the related liability
An expected insurance recovery related to a recognised loss contingency is itself a GAIN CONTINGENCY, recognised only when realisation is PROBABLE (a lower threshold specifically permitted for insurance recoveries once the underlying loss event has occurred, differing slightly from the general "realised or realisable" gain contingency threshold). The recovery is generally NOT netted against the related liability on the balance sheet unless a genuine RIGHT OF SETOFF exists – cross-referencing the general offsetting principles discussed in the ASC 210 poster.

Worked Example – Loss Contingency at the Minimum of a Range

Applying the minimum-of-range rule
Example: Pending litigation with an estimated loss range
An entity's legal counsel estimates a probable loss from pending litigation will fall somewhere between $2,000,000 and $5,000,000, with no amount within that range considered a better estimate than any other.
Under ASC 450, the entity accrues $2,000,000 (the minimum of the range), with the remaining $3,000,000 potential exposure disclosed in the notes. Under IAS 37, if this were treated as a discrete single obligation, the entity might instead accrue its BEST estimate of the most likely outcome within the range (which could be a different figure entirely, possibly closer to the middle of the range) – producing a potentially quite different accrued liability for the SAME underlying facts.
11

Worked Example – a Gain Contingency Left Unrecognised

A probable, but not yet realised, favourable lawsuit outcome
Example: An entity as plaintiff in a strong case
An entity is the plaintiff in a lawsuit against a supplier for breach of contract. Legal counsel believes the entity is PROBABLE to prevail and recover approximately $3,000,000, but the case has not yet been decided or settled.
NO gain is recognised in the financial statements, despite legal counsel's PROBABLE assessment – the gain is not yet REALISED or REALISABLE. The entity may DISCLOSE the nature of the contingency in the notes, but the $3,000,000 does not appear anywhere on the face of the financial statements until the case is actually resolved in the entity's favour.

ASC 450 vs IAS 37 – Comprehensive Comparison

Comparison – ASC 450 vs IAS 37
FeatureUS GAAP (ASC 450)IFRS (IAS 37)
Loss recognition thresholdPROBABLE and reasonably estimablePROBABLE (interpreted as more likely than not) outflow, reliably estimable – broadly similar in spirit
Measurement of a rangeMINIMUM of the range, if no better estimate existsEXPECTED VALUE (for large populations) or MOST LIKELY outcome (for single obligations) – often producing a HIGHER measured liability than the US minimum-of-range approach
Gain/contingent asset recognitionRealised or realisableVirtually certain
Constructive obligation conceptNot a general feature of this Topic (restructuring timing addressed separately, more restrictively, under ASC 420)Central to IAS 37 – a detailed, announced plan alone can create a provision
Onerous contractsNo general, explicit onerous contract provision requirementExplicit requirement to recognise a provision for onerous contracts
The measurement difference for a range of loss (minimum-of-range vs expected value/most likely outcome) is a genuinely underappreciated but consequential divergence – it can produce a MATERIALLY smaller accrued liability under US GAAP than IFRS for the identical underlying litigation or contingency facts.
