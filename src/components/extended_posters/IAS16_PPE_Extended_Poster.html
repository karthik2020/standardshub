Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why PPE is an asset under the Framework
An item qualifies as an asset under the Conceptual Framework if it is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events, with the potential to produce economic benefits.
HOW PPE MAPS TO THE FRAMEWORK'S ELEMENTS
Control — the entity can direct the use of the asset and obtain substantially all the remaining benefits (ownership is common but not required — e.g. a right-of-use asset under IFRS 16 is also "PPE-like" but is governed by IFRS 16) Past event — typically the purchase, construction, or exchange transaction Future economic benefit — the capacity to generate cash flows, directly or indirectly, through use in production, rental, or administration
IAS 16 operationalises the Framework's general asset definition into specific, industry-tested recognition and measurement rules for tangible long-term operating assets.
Measurement principles applied by IAS 16
The Conceptual Framework lists several possible measurement bases; IAS 16 selects specific ones for initial and subsequent measurement.
Framework basisUsed by IAS 16 for…
Historical costInitial recognition (always)
Current value (fair value)Revaluation model (optional policy)
Value in use / FVLCDRecoverable amount under IAS 36 (impairment)
ℹ️ Qualitative characteristics in action: the choice between cost and revaluation models is itself a trade-off between faithful representation (cost is verifiable and free from estimation bias) and relevance (revalued amounts better reflect current economic reality for users).
2

Scope & Applicability

What IAS 16 covers
DEFINITION OF PPE
Property, plant and equipment = tangible items that:
(a) are held for use in the production/supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes; AND
(b) are expected to be used during more than one period
SCOPE EXCLUSIONS — GOVERNED BY OTHER STANDARDS
PPE classified as held for sale → IFRS 5 Biological assets related to agricultural activity → IAS 41 Exploration and evaluation assets → IFRS 6 Mineral rights, reserves (oil, gas, similar non-regenerative resources) → outside scope of IAS 16 entirely Right-of-use assets (lessee) → IFRS 16 (though IAS 16's depreciation/derecognition principles are applied by analogy)
Key definitions you must know
Carrying amount
The amount at which an asset is recognised after deducting accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses.
Cost
The amount of cash/cash equivalents paid, or the fair value of other consideration given, to acquire an asset at the time of acquisition or construction.
Depreciable amount
Cost (or revalued amount) of an asset, less its residual value.
Recoverable amount
The higher of an asset's fair value less costs of disposal and its value in use (IAS 36 concept, relevant to impairment).
Residual value
The estimated amount the entity would currently obtain from disposal, after deducting estimated disposal costs, if the asset were already of the age and condition expected at the end of its useful life.

Recognition Criteria

The two-part recognition test
The cost of an item of PPE is recognised as an asset ONLY if BOTH conditions are met. This mirrors the general Framework recognition criteria (probable benefit + reliable measurement).
Condition 1 — Probable future economic benefit
It is probable that future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity.
Condition 2 — Reliable measurement
The cost of the item can be measured reliably.
⚠️ Spare parts and servicing equipment are usually expensed as used — UNLESS they meet the PPE definition (expected to be used for more than one period), in which case they are capitalised and depreciated, typically over the life of the related main asset or their own useful life if shorter.

Initial Measurement

Components of cost
COST FORMULA
Cost = Purchase price (net of discounts/rebates) + Import duties & non-refundable taxes + Directly attributable costs + Initial estimate of dismantling/restoration obligation
DIRECTLY ATTRIBUTABLE COSTS — INCLUDED
Costs of site preparation Initial delivery and handling costs Installation and assembly costs Costs of testing whether the asset functions properly (net of proceeds from selling items produced during testing) Professional fees (e.g. architects, engineers)
EXCLUDED FROM COST — EXPENSE INSTEAD
Costs of opening a new facility Costs of introducing a new product (advertising, promotion) Administration and other general overheads Initial operating losses incurred before the asset reaches planned performance Costs of relocating or reorganising entity operations
Worked example — initial cost
Example 1: Building a production line
An entity purchases machinery for $500,000 (list price), receives a 2% trade discount, pays import duty of $15,000, pays $20,000 for delivery and installation, and incurs $8,000 testing the machine (selling $2,000 of output produced during testing). It also estimates a legal obligation to dismantle the machine at the end of its life, with a present value of $25,000.
Cost = (500,000 × 0.98) + 15,000 + 20,000 + (8,000 − 2,000) + 25,000
= 490,000 + 15,000 + 20,000 + 6,000 + 25,000 = $556,000

Subsequent Measurement — Cost vs Revaluation Model

Cost model
Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation − Accumulated impairment losses
The default and simplest model. Applied consistently to an entire CLASS of PPE (e.g. all land and buildings, or all motor vehicles) — not asset-by-asset.
Revaluation model
Carrying amount is restated to fair value, with revaluations made REGULARLY ENOUGH that carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value at the reporting date.

Worked example — revaluation with reversal
Example 2: Reversal of a prior revaluation deficit
An asset originally cost $100,000. In Year 1 it was revalued down to $80,000, recognising a $20,000 loss in SOPL. In Year 2, it is revalued UP to $95,000.
Year 2 entry: total increase = 95,000 − 80,000 = $15,000.
• First $15,000 — reverses the PRIOR SOPL loss (since the deficit was $20,000 and the gain is $15,000, the entire gain is credited to SOPL, none reaches OCI in this case).
Dr PPE $15,000 / Cr Gain on revaluation (SOPL) $15,000.
✕ Always check the SAME asset's revaluation history FIRST before posting a new movement — mixing up the direction (treating a reversal as a fresh gain straight to OCI) is the most common revaluation error in exams and practice alike.

Depreciation

Depreciation mechanics & methods
STRAIGHT-LINE
Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
PERMITTED METHODS
Straight-line — constant charge over useful life Reducing (diminishing) balance — decreasing charge, applies a fixed % to the NET book value each year Units of production — based on expected use/output (e.g. machine hours, units produced)
⚠️ Land is NOT depreciated (unlimited useful life) — but BUILDINGS on that land ARE depreciated, even when acquired together. They must be accounted for and depreciated separately.
Worked example — components & depreciation
Example 3: Componentisation
An aircraft costs $50m. The engines (a significant component) cost $15m and have a useful life of 8 years before requiring replacement; the airframe (remaining $35m) has a useful life of 20 years.
Engines: $15m ÷ 8 years = $1.875m p.a.
Airframe: $35m ÷ 20 years = $1.75m p.a.
Total annual depreciation = $3.625m (NOT $50m ÷ a single blended life)
When the engines are replaced after 8 years, their remaining carrying amount (zero, if fully depreciated on schedule) is derecognised and the new engine cost is capitalised as a new component.

Impairment Linkage (IAS 36)

How IAS 16 and IAS 36 interact
IAS 16 itself does not contain impairment rules — whenever an indicator of impairment exists, IAS 36 is applied to determine whether the asset's carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount.

Worked example — disposal with revaluation surplus
Example 4: Disposing of a revalued asset
A revalued asset has a carrying amount of $40,000, a revaluation surplus balance of $8,000, and is sold for $46,000.
Gain on disposal = 46,000 − 40,000 = $6,000 → SOPL
Separately: Dr Revaluation surplus $8,000 / Cr Retained earnings $8,000 (transfer within equity, bypasses SOPL)
The two entries are independent — the SOPL gain reflects the sale itself; the equity transfer simply "realises" the surplus that was always sitting in OCI.

Critical Judgments & Estimates

Where management judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT ESTIMATES (IAS 8 — reviewed annually)
Useful life of each asset/component Residual value Depreciation method (reflecting consumption pattern)
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Whether a cost is capital (capitalise) or revenue (expense) in nature — particularly for subsequent expenditure (repair vs improvement) Identifying significant components requiring separate depreciation Whether commercial substance exists in an asset exchange
⚠️ Changes in useful life, residual value, or depreciation method are changes in accounting ESTIMATE under IAS 8 — applied PROSPECTIVELY, never restating prior periods.

IFRS vs US GAAP — Key Differences

Comparison — IAS 16 vs US GAAP (ASC 360)
FeatureIFRS (IAS 16)US GAAP
Revaluation modelPermittedNOT permitted — historical cost only
ComponentisationRequired for significant partsPermitted but less commonly required in practice
Impairment reversalPermitted (except goodwill)Prohibited — never reversed
Impairment test triggerOne-step: compare CA to recoverable amountTwo-step: recoverability test (undiscounted CF) then measurement
Convergence efforts have narrowed some gaps (e.g. both frameworks now broadly agree on borrowing cost capitalisation principles), but the revaluation model and impairment reversal differences remain fundamental and frequently tested.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Componentisation
Splitting an asset into significant parts with different useful lives, depreciated separately.
Useful life
The period over which an asset is expected to be available for use, or the number of production units expected from it.
Revaluation surplus
The equity reserve (OCI) representing the cumulative excess of fair value over historical cost for a revalued asset.
Fair value
The price that would be received to sell an asset in an orderly transaction between market participants (IFRS 13 definition).
Recoverable amount
Higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (IAS 36).
Class of assets
A grouping of assets of similar nature/use (e.g. land, buildings, machinery, vehicles) used for disclosure and policy application.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position (balance sheet) SOPL Statement of profit or loss (income statement) OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
