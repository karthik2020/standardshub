Objective, Scope & Definitions (paras. 1–9)

Objective & scope
Prescribes the accounting treatment and disclosures for agricultural activity — biological assets (except bearer plants) and agricultural produce AT THE POINT OF HARVEST.
Land related to agricultural activity (IPSAS 16 / IPSAS 45) Bearer plants (IPSAS 45) — though produce ON those plants is within scope Intangible assets related to agricultural activity (IPSAS 31) Biological assets held for SERVICE provision/supply Right-of-use assets from a lease of agricultural land (IPSAS 43)
Processing AFTER harvest is out of scope — e.g. turning grapes into wine. IPSAS 12 (or another applicable Standard) governs from that point (para. 5).
Key definitions (para. 9)
Agricultural activity
Managing biological transformation and harvest of biological assets for sale, distribution at no/nominal charge, or conversion into produce/additional biological assets.
Biological asset
A living animal or plant.
Agricultural produce
The HARVESTED produce of the entity's biological assets.
Bearer plant
A living plant used in producing/supplying agricultural produce, expected to bear produce for MORE THAN ONE PERIOD, with a REMOTE likelihood of being sold as produce itself.

Biological Assets, Agricultural Produce & Bearer Plants (paras. 6, 9A–11)

The biological-asset-to-product chain
Biological assetAgricultural producePost-harvest product
SheepWoolYarn, carpet
Dairy cattleMilkCheese
Trees in a timber plantationFelled treesLogs, lumber
Grape vines (bearer plant)Picked grapesWine
Tea bushes (bearer plant)Picked leavesTea
Grape vines, tea bushes, oil palms, and rubber trees usually meet the bearer plant definition (governed by IPSAS 45) — but the PRODUCE growing ON them (grapes, tea leaves, latex) stays within IPSAS 27's scope.
What is NOT a bearer plant (para. 9A)
Plants cultivated to be HARVESTED as produce themselves (e.g. lumber trees) Plants with more than a remote likelihood the entity will ALSO harvest and sell the plant itself as produce (e.g. fruit-and-lumber trees) Annual crops (e.g. maize, wheat)
Incidental SCRAP sales (e.g. firewood from a cut-down bearer plant) don't disqualify a plant from bearer-plant status (para. 9B). Three common features of agricultural activity: CAPABILITY to change, MANAGEMENT of that change, and MEASUREMENT of the change (para. 10) — this is what distinguishes it from unmanaged harvesting like ocean fishing.

Recognition & Measurement (paras. 13–29)

Recognition — three conditions (para. 13)
The entity CONTROLS the asset as a result of past events PROBABLE that future economic benefits/service potential will flow to the entity Fair value or COST can be measured reliably
Control may be evidenced by legal ownership plus branding/marking on acquisition, birth, or weaning (para. 15).
The core measurement rule
Biological asset → FAIR VALUE LESS COSTS TO SELL, on initial recognition AND at every reporting date
Agricultural produce → FAIR VALUE LESS COSTS TO SELL, AT THE POINT OF HARVEST (this becomes its "cost" under IPSAS 12 going forward)
Applies EVEN to non-exchange acquisitions (para. 17). Sale contracts do NOT adjust fair value — fair value reflects current market conditions, not contracted prices (para. 20); an unfavorable contract may instead be an ONEROUS contract under IPSAS 19.
When cost approximates fair value (para. 28) & combined-asset valuation
Little biological transformation since initial cost (e.g. seedlings just planted, newly acquired livestock) The impact of transformation on price isn't expected to be material (e.g. early growth in a 30-year pine plantation cycle)
Biological assets attached to land (e.g. plantation trees) may lack a separate market — but if an active market exists for the COMBINED package (biological assets + raw land + improvements), the fair value of raw land/improvements can be DEDUCTED from the combined fair value to isolate the biological assets' fair value (para. 29). Financing cash flows and re-establishment costs (e.g. replanting after harvest) are excluded from measurement (para. 26).

Gains & Losses (paras. 30–33)

Always through surplus or deficit — never net assets/equity
Gain/loss on INITIAL RECOGNITION of a biological asset, AND on every subsequent fair-value CHANGE → surplus or deficit, in the period it arises
WHY A LOSS CAN ARISE ON INITIAL RECOGNITION
Because COSTS TO SELL are deducted in arriving at fair value less costs to sell — day-one costs create a day-one loss.
WHY A GAIN CAN ARISE ON INITIAL RECOGNITION
E.g. when a calf is born — a new asset appears at fair value less costs to sell, with no offsetting cost basis.
The same treatment applies to agricultural produce at the point of harvest — a gain or loss on ITS initial recognition also goes through surplus or deficit (paras. 32–33).
5

Inability to Measure Fair Value Reliably (paras. 34–36)

A rebuttable presumption — narrow, and only on initial recognition
There is a PRESUMPTION that fair value can be reliably measured for a biological asset. This presumption can be rebutted ONLY on initial recognition, and only where NO quoted market prices are available AND alternative fair value measurements are CLEARLY UNRELIABLE.
In that narrow case → measure at COST less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses instead Once fair value becomes reliably measurable → switch to fair value less costs to sell Once a non-current biological asset meets IPSAS 44 held-for-sale criteria → fair value is PRESUMED measurable (para. 34)
⚠️ The presumption can be rebutted ONLY at initial recognition — an entity that has ALREADY measured a biological asset at fair value less costs to sell continues doing so until disposal, even if measurement later becomes harder (para. 35). Agricultural PRODUCE at the point of harvest has NO such exception — its fair value less costs to sell is ALWAYS considered reliably measurable (para. 36).

Disclosure — General & Descriptive (paras. 38–44)

Mandatory quantitative & descriptive disclosures
Aggregate gain/loss on initial recognition of biological assets AND agricultural produce, and from fair-value changes in biological assets A description of biological assets distinguishing CONSUMABLE vs BEARER, and assets held for SALE vs DISTRIBUTION at no/nominal charge The nature of activities involving each group of biological assets (if not disclosed elsewhere) Non-financial physical-quantity measures/estimates: each biological asset group at period-end, and produce output during the period
Consumable vs bearer — and encouraged extras
CategoryExamples
ConsumableLivestock for meat, crops (maize/wheat), produce on a bearer plant, trees grown for lumber
BearerBreeding stock, dairy/wool-producing livestock, fruit trees, vines/shrubs harvested for nuts/sap/resin
Distinguishing MATURE vs IMMATURE biological assets is ENCOURAGED (not required) — helpful for assessing timing of future cash flows/service potential; the basis for that distinction is disclosed if used (paras. 41–43).

Reconciliation of Biological Asset Carrying Amounts (paras. 47–51)

Restrictions, commitments & risk (para. 47)
Existence/carrying amounts of biological assets with RESTRICTED title, and those PLEDGED as security Nature and extent of restrictions on use or ability to sell Commitments for developing/acquiring biological assets Financial risk management strategies related to agricultural activity
The mandatory opening-to-closing reconciliation (para. 48)
Gain/loss from fair-value-less-costs-to-sell changes — SEPARATED for bearer vs consumable biological assets Increases: purchases; non-exchange acquisitions; public sector combinations Decreases: sales / held-for-sale classification (IPSAS 44); distributions at no/nominal charge; harvest Net exchange differences from translation; other changes
Separately disclosing PHYSICAL vs PRICE changes within the fair-value movement is ENCOURAGED — most useful where the production cycle exceeds one year (para. 49). Material revenue/expense items from climatic, disease, or other natural risk events (disease outbreak, flood, drought, insect plague) are disclosed per IPSAS 1 (para. 51).

Current Value Measurement Disclosures & Fair Value Unmeasurable (paras. 46A–54)

Fair value hierarchy disclosures (paras. 46A–46C)
Mirrors the IPSAS 12/16 fair value disclosure package: for agricultural assets measured at fair value (including via IPSAS 46), disclose measurement techniques/inputs, and — for Level 3 recurring measurements — the effect on surplus/deficit or net assets/equity.
Fair value measurement at period end (recurring/non-recurring), with reasons for non-recurring measurements The fair value HIERARCHY LEVEL (1, 2, or 3) For Level 3 or unobservable-input measurements: technique(s) used, and any technique CHANGE with reasons
Additional disclosures where fair value cannot be measured reliably (paras. 52–54)
A description of the biological assets concerned An explanation of why fair value cannot be reliably measured If possible, the range of estimates within which fair value is highly likely to lie
If fair value SUBSEQUENTLY becomes reliably measurable for such assets, disclose: a description, why it became measurable, and the effect of the change (para. 54).

Relationship to IAS/IFRS — Comparison with IAS 41

Comparison — IPSAS 27 vs IAS 41
IPSAS 27 is drawn primarily from IAS 41 (2001), Agriculture, as amended up to 31 December 2008.
TopicDifference
"Agricultural activity" scopeIPSAS 27 includes no/nominal-charge DISTRIBUTION transactions; IAS 41 does not address these
Service-provision biological assetsIPSAS 27 explicitly clarifies exclusion; IAS 41 has no equivalent clarification
Government grantsIAS 41 includes grant requirements; IPSAS 27 does NOT — governed instead by IPSAS 47
Non-exchange acquisitionIPSAS 27 has specific initial/subsequent measurement requirements; no IAS 41 equivalent (non-exchange transactions are rare in the private sector)
Restriction disclosureIPSAS 27 adds a specific disclosure requirement for use/sale restrictions
Consumable/bearer disclosureIPSAS 27 REQUIRES the quantified distinction; IAS 41 only ENCOURAGES it
Terminology"Surplus or deficit," "statement of financial performance" (IPSAS 27) vs "profit or loss," "statement of comprehensive income" (IAS 41)

Statement / Process Mapping

Recognition & measurement decision map
1. Is this a LIVING ANIMAL OR PLANT used/managed for agricultural activity? If it's land, an intangible, or a bearer plant itself → out of scope (§1) 2. Does it meet the recognition criteria — control, probable benefit flow, reliable measurement? (§3) 3. Is it a BIOLOGICAL ASSET (pre-harvest) or AGRICULTURAL PRODUCE (at point of harvest)? Both → fair value less costs to sell (§3) 4. Can fair value be reliably measured? Presumed YES, rebuttable only on initial recognition for biological assets (never for produce) → if rebutted, use cost less depreciation/impairment instead (§5) 5. Recognize every gain/loss — initial recognition AND every subsequent fair value change — in SURPLUS OR DEFICIT (§4) 6. Present the mandatory reconciliation of opening to closing carrying amounts, separated for bearer vs consumable assets (§7)

Quick-Reference Glossary

Biological transformation
Growth, degeneration, production, and procreation processes causing qualitative or quantitative changes in a biological asset.
Harvest
The detachment of produce from a biological asset, or the cessation of a biological asset's life processes.
Costs to sell
Incremental costs directly attributable to an asset's disposal, excluding finance costs and income taxes.
Group of biological assets
An aggregation of similar living animals or plants.
Consumable biological assets
Held for harvest as produce, or for sale/distribution as biological assets themselves.
Bearer biological assets
Used repeatedly/continuously for more than one year to bear produce, rather than being produce themselves.
