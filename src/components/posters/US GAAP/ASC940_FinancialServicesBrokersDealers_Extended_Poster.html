Conceptual Framework Linkage

A trading-first business model, reflected in trading-first accounting
Broker-dealers exist to TRADE securities — buying and selling on their own account and on behalf of customers, often within the SAME trading day. ASC 940 reflects this by recognising transactions at TRADE DATE (not settlement date) and by measuring virtually ALL trading securities at FAIR VALUE through earnings, rather than applying the tiered AFS/HTM/amortised-cost classification scheme used by most other entities holding financial instruments.
2

Scope

Registered broker-dealers in securities
ASC 940 applies to entities registered as BROKER-DEALERS under securities laws, engaging in the purchase and sale of securities for their own account and as agent for customers.
3

Trade Date vs Settlement Date Accounting

Recognition at the moment of the trade, not settlement
Broker-dealers record securities transactions on a TRADE DATE basis — recognising the purchase/sale (and any resulting gain/loss) on the date the TRANSACTION is EXECUTED, rather than waiting for the later SETTLEMENT date (typically 1-2 business days later) when cash and securities actually change hands. This reflects that economic exposure to price movements begins at the moment of the trade itself.
4

Trading Securities — Universal Fair Value Through Earnings

No AFS/HTM tiering — trading securities are simply marked to market
Broker-dealers' securities held for TRADING purposes are measured at FAIR VALUE, with changes recognised DIRECTLY in EARNINGS — WITHOUT the tiered available-for-sale/held-to-maturity classification framework other entities apply under ASC 320/321. This reflects the fundamentally TRADING-oriented nature of a broker-dealer's securities inventory, which exists to be actively bought and sold rather than held for yield or long-term investment purposes.
5

Receivables From and Payables To Customers and Broker-Dealers

Reflecting the mechanics of clearing and settlement
Receivables from and payables to CUSTOMERS (e.g. margin balances) and other BROKER-DEALERS (amounts arising from unsettled trades, clearing organization deposits) are separately presented, reflecting the distinctive counterparty structure of the securities clearing and settlement process.
6

Fails to Deliver / Fails to Receive

Unsettled trades where the counterparty hasn't yet performed
A "FAIL TO DELIVER" arises when a broker-dealer has SOLD securities but has not yet DELIVERED them to the buyer by the expected settlement date; a "FAIL TO RECEIVE" arises when it has BOUGHT securities but not yet RECEIVED them. These are presented as RECEIVABLES/PAYABLES reflecting the unsettled trade position, requiring ongoing monitoring given regulatory settlement requirements.
7

Customer Protection — Segregation of Assets

Regulatory-driven asset segregation, reflected in presentation
Broker-dealers are subject to regulatory requirements (e.g. SEC Rule 15c3-3) to SEGREGATE customer cash and securities from the firm's OWN proprietary assets, to protect customers in the event of the broker-dealer's insolvency — this segregation is reflected in the presentation and disclosure of customer-related assets and liabilities.
8

Worked Example — Trade Date Recognition

Recognising the trade before settlement occurs
Example: A securities purchase executed near period-end
A broker-dealer executes a $5,000,000 securities purchase on December 30 (trade date), with settlement not occurring until January 2 (settlement date) of the following year.
The broker-dealer recognises the $5,000,000 securities purchase and corresponding payable on December 30 (trade date) — INCLUDED in the CURRENT year's balance sheet — even though cash and securities won't actually change hands until January 2 of the following year.
9

Worked Example — a Fail-to-Deliver Position

An unsettled sale
Example: Securities sold but not yet delivered
A broker-dealer sells $2,000,000 of securities to a counterparty, but due to a settlement delay, has not yet delivered the securities by the expected settlement date.
The broker-dealer records a $2,000,000 receivable representing the "FAIL TO DELIVER" position — reflecting that it has sold the securities (and is owed payment) but has not yet completed physical/electronic delivery, requiring ongoing monitoring until the fail is resolved.

No Dedicated IFRS Broker-Dealer Standard — Discussion

General IFRS 9 FVTPL classification achieves a similar outcome
IFRS has NO dedicated broker-dealer industry standard. A broker-dealer reporting under IFRS would classify trading securities as measured at FAIR VALUE THROUGH PROFIT OR LOSS (FVTPL) under GENERAL IFRS 9 classification principles (since they are held for trading, they fail the "hold to collect" business model tests) — arriving at a BROADLY similar practical outcome to ASC 940's universal fair-value-through-earnings treatment, without a dedicated industry standard on either side. Trade date vs settlement date accounting is ALSO addressed under general IFRS 9 principles, which explicitly permit EITHER trade date or settlement date accounting as an accounting POLICY choice — broadly converged, though IFRS 9 offers this as an explicit choice rather than the closer-to-mandatory trade date convention common in US broker-dealer practice.

Recognition of a securities transaction on the date the trade is executed, rather than the later settlement date.
Fail to deliver / fail to receive
An unsettled securities trade where the broker-dealer has not yet delivered securities sold, or received securities purchased, by the expected settlement date.
