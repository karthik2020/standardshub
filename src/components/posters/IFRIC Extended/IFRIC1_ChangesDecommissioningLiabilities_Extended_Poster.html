1

Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why IFRIC 1 exists as a bridge interpretation
IAS 16 requires the initial estimate of decommissioning, restoration, and similar obligations to be capitalised as part of an asset's cost; IAS 37 governs how that provision is subsequently remeasured. Neither standard, on its own, specified what happens to the ASSET when the LIABILITY changes after initial recognition — IFRIC 1 closes that gap.
Without IFRIC 1, entities could faithfully apply IAS 16 and IAS 37 individually yet reach inconsistent answers on where a subsequent remeasurement of the liability should land — asset cost, equity, or profit or loss.
Faithful representation of a genuinely two-sided event
A change in a decommissioning estimate is not simply an income statement event — it originates in a re-estimation of a future cash outflow that is directly linked to an asset already capitalised. IFRIC 1's cost-model mechanics preserve the historical-cost linkage between asset and liability wherever that remains a faithful representation, and default to profit or loss only where capitalisation would no longer depict an economic reality (e.g. an asset with a zero or negative carrying amount).
2

Scope & Applicability

What IFRIC 1 covers
IFRIC 1 applies to changes in the measurement of any existing decommissioning, restoration, or similar liability that is BOTH recognised as a provision under IAS 37 AND included as part of the cost of an item of property, plant and equipment under IAS 16 (or, by extension, a right-of-use asset or similar capitalised cost base).
TYPICAL FACT PATTERNS
Nuclear plant decommissioning obligationsMine site restoration and rehabilitation obligationsOil and gas platform removal obligationsLeasehold restoration obligations linked to a right-of-use asset
Key definitions
Decommissioning, restoration and similar liability
A provision recognised under IAS 37 for the estimated cost of dismantling, removing, or restoring an item of property, plant and equipment, capitalised as part of that asset's cost under IAS 16.
Unwinding of the discount
The periodic increase in the provision that reflects the passage of time, since the liability is initially measured at a discounted, present-value amount.
3

The Three Change Drivers

What can cause the liability to move after initial recognition
DriverDescription
Change in estimated outflowA revision to the estimated amount or timing of the resources required to settle the obligation (e.g. updated engineering cost estimates, revised closure date)
Change in the discount rateA change in the current market-based discount rate used to measure the provision, including changes arising purely from the passage of time affecting the rate applicable to the remaining cash flows
Unwinding of the discountThe routine increase in the liability reflecting the passage of time — NOT a remeasurement in the same sense as the first two, and always recognised in profit or loss (see Section 6)
⚠️ Only the first two drivers are "changes in measurement" that IFRIC 1's asset-adjustment mechanics apply to. The unwinding of the discount is deliberately carved out and always hits profit or loss as a finance cost.
4

Cost Model Treatment (with worked example)

The core mechanic
Under the cost model, a change in the liability is added to, or deducted from, the cost of the related asset in the current period. The adjusted depreciable amount is then depreciated PROSPECTIVELY over the asset's remaining useful life — no restatement of prior depreciation.
CEILING RULE
The amount deducted from asset cost cannot exceed the asset's carrying amount. If a decrease in the liability would exceed the carrying amount, the excess is recognised IMMEDIATELY in profit or loss.
Worked example — upward revision of a closure estimate
Example: Increase in decommissioning estimate
A mining company's asset has a carrying amount of $2,400,000 and 8 years of remaining useful life. An updated environmental survey increases the present value of the restoration obligation by $360,000.
Entry: Debit Asset cost $360,000 / Credit Decommissioning provision $360,000.
Revised carrying amount = $2,760,000, depreciated over the remaining 8 years — no adjustment to prior periods' depreciation expense.
5

Revalued Asset Treatment (with worked example)

The core mechanic
For assets carried under the revaluation model, a change in the liability alters the revaluation surplus or deficit previously recognised on that specific asset. A DECREASE in the liability is credited to other comprehensive income and increases the revaluation surplus — UNLESS it reverses a revaluation deficit previously recognised in profit or loss, in which case it is recognised in profit or loss first.
INCREASE IN THE LIABILITY
Recognised in profit or loss, EXCEPT to the extent there is a credit balance in the revaluation surplus for that asset, in which case it is recognised in other comprehensive income and reduces the surplus
Worked example — revalued asset, increase in liability
Example: Increase absorbed partly by revaluation surplus
A revalued oil platform asset carries a revaluation surplus of $150,000. A change in discount rate increases the decommissioning provision by $200,000.
$150,000 of the increase is debited against the existing revaluation surplus (OCI); the remaining $50,000 is recognised as an expense in profit or loss. The revaluation surplus for this asset is reduced to nil.
⚠️ A revaluation surplus/deficit adjustment under IFRIC 1 may also indicate the whole class of assets needs to be revalued, to ensure the carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value — triggering a broader IAS 16 revaluation exercise.
6

The Unwinding of Discount

Always a finance cost, never capitalised
The periodic increase in the provision that reflects the passage of time is recognised in profit or loss as a finance cost AS IT OCCURS — it is explicitly excluded from the asset-cost adjustment mechanics that apply to genuine remeasurements. This preserves a clean distinction between (a) the cost of the obligation itself, which is capitalised, and (b) the time-value-of-money cost of carrying that obligation, which is an ongoing financing charge.
TYPICAL ENTRY EACH PERIOD
Debit Finance cost (profit or loss) / Credit Decommissioning provision — for the discount unwinding amount only
7

Ceiling Tests — When the Decrease Exceeds Carrying Amount

What happens when the asset "runs out of room"
Because a decommissioning asset is depreciated over time while the related liability can still be revised upward or downward, it is possible for a downward revision to the liability to exceed the asset's remaining carrying amount — particularly late in an asset's life. In that case, the amount that CANNOT be absorbed into asset cost (because it would drive the carrying amount negative) is recognised IMMEDIATELY in profit or loss instead.
Example: Decrease exceeding carrying amount
An asset nearing the end of its useful life has a carrying amount of $40,000. A favourable revision to closure cost estimates reduces the provision by $65,000.
$40,000 reduces the asset's carrying amount to nil; the remaining $25,000 is recognised immediately as a gain in profit or loss.
8

Interaction with Impairment (IAS 36)

A capitalised increase can itself trigger an impairment test
Because an upward revision to a decommissioning liability increases asset cost without necessarily increasing the asset's recoverable amount, entities must consider whether the resulting higher carrying amount is an indicator of impairment under IAS 36, and test accordingly. This is a common area of practical judgement, particularly for long-life assets in commodity industries where recoverable amounts are already sensitive to price assumptions.

IFRIC 1 vs US GAAP (ASC 410)

Comparison — IFRIC 1 vs ASC 410 (Asset Retirement Obligations)
FeatureIFRIC 1 (IFRS)US GAAP (ASC 410)
Discount rate on remeasurementCurrent market-based rate applied to the WHOLE revised liabilityA "layered" approach — each upward revision is discounted at the CURRENT credit-adjusted risk-free rate at the time of the revision and tracked as a separate layer; downward revisions use the rate that applied when the related layer was recognised
Revalued assetsExplicit mechanics linking liability changes to revaluation surplus/OCINot applicable — revaluation of PP&E is not permitted under US GAAP
Ceiling on downward asset adjustmentDecrease capped at carrying amount, excess to profit or lossBroadly similar outcome in substance, though mechanically applied through the layer approach
The layered approach under ASC 410 is the single biggest mechanical divergence — it avoids ever applying a single blended discount rate to a liability built up from estimate changes made at different points in time, which IFRIC 1's approach effectively does.

Quick-reference glossary
Revaluation surplus
The cumulative OCI balance representing the excess of an asset's revalued amount over its historical cost carrying amount, tracked per asset (or class).
Obligating event
The past event that creates a present legal or constructive obligation under IAS 37, giving rise to the decommissioning provision in the first place.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Cost model
The IAS 16 measurement model under which PP&E is carried at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment — the default model to which IFRIC 1's asset-cost-adjustment mechanics most directly apply.
Revaluation model
The alternative IAS 16 measurement model under which PP&E is carried at fair value at the date of revaluation less subsequent depreciation — triggers the OCI/revaluation-surplus mechanics under IFRIC 1 instead.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position SOPL Statement of profit or loss OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
