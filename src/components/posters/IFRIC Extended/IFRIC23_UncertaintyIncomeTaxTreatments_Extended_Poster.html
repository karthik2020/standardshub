Conceptual Framework Linkage

IAS 12 assumed certainty that rarely exists
IAS 12 sets out how to account for current and deferred tax based on tax laws and rates, largely assuming the tax treatment adopted is settled. In practice, entities often take positions in tax filings that MAY be challenged and overturned by tax authorities — creating genuine measurement uncertainty about taxable profit, tax bases, and tax rates that IAS 12 did not explicitly address. IFRIC 23 fills that gap with a structured, judgement-based framework.
A probability-weighted view of an uncertain legal outcome
Rather than requiring a binary "will it be accepted or not" determination, IFRIC 23's expected-value and most-likely-amount methods allow the FINANCIAL STATEMENTS to reflect a range of possible outcomes weighted by likelihood — a more faithful representation of genuine legal/tax uncertainty than either assuming full success or fully providing for the worst case.
2

Scope & Applicability

What IFRIC 23 covers
IFRIC 23 applies to the determination of taxable profit (tax loss), tax bases, unused tax losses, unused tax credits, and tax rates, when there is UNCERTAINTY over income tax treatments under IAS 12 — specifically, when it is unclear whether the relevant tax authority will accept a particular tax treatment an entity has used or plans to use in its income tax filings.
TYPICAL FACT PATTERNS
Transfer pricing positions that may be challenged on auditDeductibility of a specific expense category under an ambiguous tax law provisionAvailability of a tax credit or incentive subject to interpretation
Key definitions
Uncertain tax treatment
A tax treatment used or planned to be used by an entity in its income tax filings where there is uncertainty over whether the relevant tax authority will accept it under tax law.
Tax authority
The body responsible for assessing and collecting income taxes from the entity, assumed under IFRIC 23 to examine amounts reported with full knowledge of all relevant information.
3

The Core Assumption — Full Examination

Assumed scrutiny, not assumed leniency or ignorance
In assessing an uncertain tax treatment, an entity ASSUMES that the tax authority will examine the amounts it has a right to examine and has FULL KNOWLEDGE OF ALL RELEVANT INFORMATION when doing so. This deliberately rules out any assumption that a position might simply go unnoticed or unexamined — the analysis proceeds strictly on the technical merits of the position, not on the probability of detection.
4

Considering Treatments Separately or Together

Whichever approach better predicts the resolution
An entity determines whether to consider each uncertain tax treatment SEPARATELY, or TOGETHER with one or more other uncertain tax treatments, based on which approach BETTER PREDICTS the resolution of the uncertainty. Factors relevant to this determination include whether treatments are prepared and submitted together, and whether the tax authority is expected to examine and resolve them as a package versus individually.
5

Is It Probable the Treatment Will Be Accepted?

A genuine fork in the accounting
Assessment outcomeConsequence
PROBABLE the tax authority will accept the treatmentThe entity determines taxable profit, tax bases, unused tax losses/credits, and tax rates CONSISTENTLY with the tax treatment used or planned to be used in its filings — i.e. no separate uncertainty adjustment is needed
NOT probable the tax authority will accept the treatmentThe entity reflects the EFFECT of uncertainty using either the most likely amount method or the expected value method (see Section 6)
6

Measurement — Most Likely Amount vs Expected Value

Two permitted methods, chosen by predictive power
MOST LIKELY AMOUNT METHOD
The single most likely outcome in a range of possible outcomes is used — most appropriate where the possible outcomes are essentially binary (e.g. a deduction is either fully allowed or fully disallowed).
EXPECTED VALUE METHOD
The sum of the probability-weighted amounts in a range of possible outcomes is used — most appropriate where there is a RANGE of possible outcomes that are neither binary nor concentrated on one value.
The entity chooses WHICHEVER method better predicts the resolution of the uncertainty — not a fixed policy choice applied uniformly to all uncertain tax positions.
7

Worked Example — Most Likely Amount Method

A binary deductibility dispute
Example: Disputed expense deduction
An entity has claimed a $2,000,000 tax deduction for a restructuring cost, taking the position it qualifies under a specific tax provision. Management assesses a 35% probability the position will be fully accepted (full $2,000,000 deduction) and a 65% probability it will be fully rejected (nil deduction) on audit — a genuinely binary outcome, since the tax provision's application does not admit a partial result.
Because the outcome is BINARY, the MOST LIKELY AMOUNT method is used: since rejection (65%) is more likely than acceptance (35%), the entity reflects NIL deduction for this uncertain position in determining current tax, and the resulting difference from its filed position is reflected as an uncertain tax liability (or a reduction to a tax asset).
8

Reassessment of Judgments and Estimates

Not a one-time determination
An entity REASSESSES its judgements and estimates if FACTS AND CIRCUMSTANCES CHANGE, or as a result of NEW INFORMATION that affects the judgements or estimates — for example, actions by the tax authority, changes in tax law, or the expiry of the tax authority's right to examine or re-examine a particular tax treatment. This ensures the accounting for uncertain tax positions remains current with the evolving state of information available, rather than fixed at initial recognition.

IFRIC 23 vs US GAAP

Comparison — IFRIC 23 vs US GAAP (ASC 740-10, formerly FIN 48)
FeatureIFRS (IFRIC 23)US GAAP (ASC 740-10)
Recognition thresholdNo separate two-step recognition test — measurement directly reflects the probability of acceptanceA distinct TWO-STEP model: first, a "more likely than not" RECOGNITION threshold must be cleared before any benefit is recognised at all; second, MEASUREMENT of the recognised amount uses a cumulative probability approach
Measurement approachMost likely amount OR expected value, chosen by predictive powerLargest amount that is MORE THAN 50% likely to be realised upon settlement (cumulative probability), a narrower prescribed approach than IFRIC 23's flexible choice
Overall structureSingle-step, flexible measurement frameworkTwo-step recognition-then-measurement framework — a more mechanically prescriptive model
This is one of the more STRUCTURALLY different areas between the frameworks: US GAAP's FIN 48-derived two-step recognition threshold has no direct IFRS equivalent — IFRIC 23 folds uncertainty directly into measurement without a separate initial recognition hurdle, which can produce different recognised tax positions for economically similar uncertain treatments.

Quick-reference glossary
Most likely amount method
A measurement approach that uses the single most probable outcome from a range of possibilities, most suited to essentially binary uncertain tax positions.
Expected value method
A measurement approach using the probability-weighted sum of a range of possible outcomes, most suited to uncertain tax positions with a genuine spread of possible results.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Taxable profit (tax loss)
The profit or loss for a period, determined in accordance with tax authority rules, upon which income taxes are payable or recoverable — directly affected by how uncertain tax treatments are reflected under IFRIC 23.
Probable
More likely than not, in the sense generally used across IFRS — the threshold IFRIC 23 uses to determine whether an entity's filed tax position can be used directly, without a separate uncertainty adjustment.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position SOPL Statement of profit or loss OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
