HGB §335 — Festsetzung von Ordnungsgeld (Imposition of a Coercive Fine)
Extended reference poster · Book 3, Section 2, Subsection 6 · The actual enforcement mechanism behind §325's disclosure obligations, referenced repeatedly throughout this series · German commercial law

Conceptual Framework Linkage

Where §325's disclosure obligation gets genuine teeth
§325 required disclosure; §329 required a formal completeness check; §335 supplies what actually HAPPENS when a company fails to comply — a structured, escalating coercive-fine procedure administered by the Bundesamt für Justiz. Given how many German Kapitalgesellschaften are small, ordinary businesses rather than sophisticated public-market issuers, this is likely the single MOST frequently applied enforcement provision this entire Straf-/Bußgeld-/Ordnungsgeld Subsection contains.
HOW §335 MAPS TO GERMAN ACCOUNTING PRINCIPLES
Proportionality, once again — the size-sensitive fine reduction for late-but-eventual compliance continues the SAME cost-benefit theme running throughout this entire poster series Compliance-forcing, not merely punitive — the escalating "repeat the order, threaten a further fine" mechanism is designed to genuinely COMPEL disclosure, not simply punish its absence once and move on

Scope — Representative Body Members AND the Company Itself

A dual target, unlike most other provisions in this cluster
§335 proceedings can be directed against the members of the Vertretungsorgan (representative body — the management board or equivalent) personally, and SEPARATELY, the SAME proceeding may ALSO be conducted directly against the Kapitalgesellschaft itself — a genuinely dual-track approach not shared by every other provision this cluster has covered.
3

§335(1) — the Basic Mechanism

A structured, multi-stage coercive-fine proceeding
§335(1) provides that where representative body members fail to comply with the §325/§325a disclosure obligations established earlier in this series, the Bundesamt für Justiz conducts an Ordnungsgeldverfahren under §335(2)-(6) — a structured proceeding, not a single automatic fine, involving formal warning, an opportunity to object, and — where non-compliance persists — actual imposition and escalating repetition.
4

"No Escape via Your Own Earlier Non-Compliance"

You cannot use your own prior failure to shield yourself from this one
§335(1) sentence 3 provides that the proceeding is NOT blocked merely because an EARLIER, PREREQUISITE obligation — such as actually PREPARING the Jahresabschluss/Konzernabschluss in the first place, or promptly engaging an auditor — has itself not yet been fulfilled. A company cannot escape or delay the disclosure-enforcement proceeding by pointing to its own even-more-fundamental failure to have prepared the underlying documents at all.
5

Worked Example — a Company That Never Even Prepared Its Accounts

Compounding failures don't compound excuses
Example 1: A company that missed both preparation and disclosure deadlines
A medium-sized GmbH has failed not only to disclose its Jahresabschluss within the §325(1a) twelve-month deadline, but has never even PREPARED it in the first place, and has not engaged an auditor at all.
The Bundesamt für Justiz may STILL open an Ordnungsgeldverfahren under §335(1) for the disclosure failure specifically — the company cannot argue "we couldn't disclose because we never prepared the accounts" as a defence; §335(1) sentence 3 explicitly forecloses that argument, ensuring a company cannot escape enforcement simply by failing even more comprehensively.
6

§335(1d) — the BaFin Notification Loop for Listed Companies

A genuine regulatory feedback loop, not merely an isolated enforcement process
§335(1d) requires the Bundesamt to notify BaFin (the Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht, Germany's financial regulator) IMMEDIATELY whenever an Ordnungsgeld is imposed against a capital-market-oriented company (per §264d) or a member of its representative body — and to further notify BaFin if an appeal (Beschwerde) is lodged against the fine, and of that appeal's ultimate outcome. This creates a genuine cross-regulatory information channel: a listed company's disclosure failure does not stay confined to the Bundesamt's own files, but reaches the securities regulator directly and automatically.
7

Worked Example — a Listed Company's Failure Reaching the Securities Regulator

An administrative fine that isn't just a quiet, isolated matter
Example 2: A listed AG's fine automatically reaching BaFin
A capital-market-oriented AG, established earlier in this series, misses its shortened §325(4) four-month disclosure deadline, and the Bundesamt imposes an Ordnungsgeld against the company.
The Bundesamt AUTOMATICALLY notifies BaFin of this fine under §335(1d) — the AG's disclosure failure becomes visible to the SECURITIES regulator, not merely to the Bundesamt's own internal process, reflecting the genuinely heightened public-market significance of a LISTED company's disclosure compliance relative to an ordinary private company's.
8

§335(4) — the Six-Week Warning, and the Escalating Repeat-Fine Structure

A genuine second chance, followed by an ongoing, repeating compliance mechanism
§335(4) sentence 1 provides that if the parties do NOT comply with the legal obligation within SIX WEEKS of receiving the formal Androhung (warning notice), or do not justify their non-compliance via a formal Einspruch (objection), the Ordnungsgeld is IMPOSED — and SIMULTANEOUSLY, the earlier order is REPEATED, with the THREAT of a FURTHER, renewed Ordnungsgeld if compliance still doesn't follow. This is a genuinely ESCALATING, ongoing mechanism designed to keep pressuring for actual compliance, rather than a single one-time punitive event.
9

Worked Example — Walking Through the Full Timeline

A multi-stage process, not a single automatic penalty
Example 3: A late-filing medium-sized GmbH's complete Ordnungsgeld journey
A medium-sized GmbH misses its ordinary 12-month filing deadline. The Bundesamt sends a formal Androhung, giving the company six weeks to comply or object. The company does neither within that period.
The Bundesamt IMPOSES the threatened Ordnungsgeld (a MINIMUM of €2,500, up to €25,000) under §335(4) sentence 1 — and SIMULTANEOUSLY repeats its original disclosure order, with a THREAT of a FURTHER Ordnungsgeld if the company still hasn't complied. If the company continues to ignore the requirement, this cycle can genuinely REPEAT — each round imposing a further fine and renewing the threat, until compliance is finally achieved.
10

The Size-Sensitive Reduction for Late-But-Eventual Compliance

Rewarding genuine, if tardy, compliance — and scaling the reward by company size
§335(4) sentence 2 provides that WHERE the company DOES fulfil the disclosure obligation, but only AFTER the six-week deadline has passed, the Bundesamt must REDUCE the fine, scaled by company size:
Company categoryReduced fine
Micro-corporation using the §326(2) Hinterlegung option€500
Small corporation (§267(1))€1,000
Any other company where a higher fine was threatened€2,500 (the statutory minimum)
A genuinely elegant, size-sensitive mitigation mechanism, continuing the SAME cost-benefit relief theme this poster series has documented since §267's own size thresholds — smaller companies that ultimately comply, even late, are treated proportionately more leniently than the ordinary escalated fine would otherwise impose.
11

Worked Example — Contrasting the Reduced Fines Across Company Sizes

The exact same lateness, three genuinely different outcomes
Example 4: Three companies, all filing exactly two weeks late, past the six-week deadline
A micro-corporation using Hinterlegung, a small corporation, and a large corporation each file their accounts two weeks after their own respective six-week Androhung deadline expired — genuinely, actually complying, just later than they should have.
The micro-corporation's fine is reduced to just €500. The small corporation's fine is reduced to €1,000. The large corporation, not qualifying for either specific reduction, faces the STATUTORY MINIMUM of €2,500 instead — the SAME two-week lateness producing a fivefold difference in outcome purely based on the company's own size classification.
12

§335(6) — Requesting Size-Classification Data Directly

A practical tool for the Bundesamt to classify companies it lacks information about
§335(6) permits the Bundesamt, where it lacks sufficient information to properly classify a company's own §267/§267a size category, to REQUIRE the company itself to provide the specific data needed — the balance sheet total (net of any negative equity per §268(3)), revenue (§277(1)), and average employee count (§267(5)) — for both the relevant year and any earlier years needed for the classification test. A genuinely practical administrative tool, ensuring the size-sensitive relief mechanisms this poster series has documented throughout can actually be APPLIED correctly, even where the Bundesamt doesn't already independently know a company's precise size.
13

§335(2) — Borrowed Procedural Mechanics

Reusing established German procedural frameworks rather than reinventing them
§335(2) applies specific provisions of the OWiG (Gesetz über Ordnungswidrigkeiten, Germany's general administrative-offences procedural code) and the FamFG (the procedural code otherwise governing family-law and voluntary-jurisdiction matters) correspondingly to the §335 Ordnungsgeldverfahren — a structural choice this poster series has now seen repeatedly (§298(1)'s own "corresponding application" borrowing of individual-accounts rules for group accounts is a conceptually similar pattern): rather than legislating an entirely bespoke procedure, HGB borrows established procedural mechanics from elsewhere in German law wherever genuinely suitable.

Critical Judgments & Practical Difficulties

Where judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Whether a formal Einspruch (objection) genuinely justifies the non-compliance, versus being a procedurally valid but substantively insufficient excuse
PRACTICAL DIFFICULTIES
Managing the genuinely escalating, repeating nature of the fine mechanism for a company that remains persistently non-compliant across multiple rounds
15

Relationship to IFRS

Disclosure enforcement sits entirely outside any accounting standard
Precisely as with the earlier disclosure-filing poster's own observation, IFRS itself has no equivalent Ordnungsgeld-style mechanism — HOW a jurisdiction actually COMPELS compliance with its own disclosure requirements is left entirely to local law, wherever IFRS is adopted. §335 completes this poster series' documentation of the practical enforcement layer sitting beneath every disclosure obligation this series has covered — the genuine mechanism ensuring §325's requirements are not merely aspirational.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Ordnungsgeldverfahren
Coercive fine proceeding — the structured, multi-stage process this provision establishes for enforcing disclosure compliance.
Androhung
The formal warning notice giving the company six weeks to comply or object before a fine is actually imposed.
Einspruch
A formal objection that may justify non-compliance within the six-week window.
BaFin
Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht — Germany's financial/securities regulator, automatically notified of fines against capital-market-oriented companies.
Vertretungsorgan
Representative body — the management board or equivalent, one of two possible §335 proceeding targets alongside the company itself.
Wiedereinsetzung
Reinstatement of a missed procedural deadline where the company was blamelessly prevented from acting in time.
