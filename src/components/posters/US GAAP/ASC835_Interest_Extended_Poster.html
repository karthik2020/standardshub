Conceptual Framework Linkage

Two genuinely different interest questions under one Topic heading
ASC 835 answers two DIFFERENT questions that both happen to involve interest: (1) should interest cost incurred while CONSTRUCTING an asset be CAPITALISED into that asset's cost rather than expensed (Sections 3-5)? and (2) when a note doesn't carry a MARKET rate of interest, how should it be recorded at a genuinely economically meaningful amount (Section 6)? Both questions share the common thread of ensuring interest is reflected at its true ECONOMIC substance rather than its stated contractual terms.
2

Scope & Applicability

Interest capitalization and imputation, two distinct subtopics
ASC 835-20 governs CAPITALISATION of interest cost during the construction/production period of QUALIFYING assets. ASC 835-30 governs IMPUTATION of an appropriate interest rate on notes and similar instruments that don't bear a market rate of interest.
3

Interest Capitalization — the Core Principle

Added to asset cost, not expensed, during the construction period
Interest cost INCURRED during the period a QUALIFYING asset is being CONSTRUCTED or PRODUCED for its intended use is CAPITALISED — added to the cost of the asset — rather than expensed as incurred, reflecting that financing costs are a genuine, unavoidable part of bringing the asset to its usable condition. IAS 23 (Borrowing Costs) similarly REQUIRES (not merely permits) capitalising borrowing costs directly attributable to qualifying assets — a broadly converged CORE principle, though the specific computational MECHANICS differ (Section 5).
4

Qualifying Assets

Substantial time to get ready, with a routine-inventory exclusion
A QUALIFYING asset is one that requires a SUBSTANTIAL PERIOD of time to get ready for its INTENDED USE — assets under construction for an entity's own use, or discrete projects such as real estate developments. Inventory that is ROUTINELY manufactured in LARGE QUANTITIES on a REPETITIVE basis is SPECIFICALLY EXCLUDED (not a qualifying asset) — the same general exclusion IAS 23 applies for inventories produced in large quantities on a repetitive basis.
5

Computing the Avoidable Interest / Capitalization Rate

A genuine mechanical difference, despite the converged core principle
ASC 835 computes capitalised interest using an "AVOIDABLE INTEREST" concept — applying a WEIGHTED AVERAGE of rates on the entity's OUTSTANDING borrowings (not necessarily tied to any SPECIFIC borrowing for the project) if there's no dedicated new borrowing for the qualifying asset. IAS 23 more directly TIES the capitalised amount to SPECIFIC borrowings for the asset where identifiable (using the actual rate on that specific debt), falling back to a weighted average capitalisation rate for GENERAL borrowings only when a specific borrowing isn't identifiable. Both frameworks apply broadly similar "avoidable interest" LOGIC, but the SPECIFIC computational sequencing and terminology differ somewhat between the two.
6

Imputation of Interest (835-30)

Recording notes at genuine present value, not stated face value
When a NOTE receivable or payable does NOT bear a MARKET rate of interest (e.g. a non-interest-bearing note, or one with a below-market stated rate), the note is recorded at its PRESENT VALUE, discounted using an IMPUTED market rate of interest — with the DIFFERENCE between the note's FACE value and its present value treated as a discount (or premium), AMORTISED as interest income/expense over the note's life. A broadly similar imputation principle exists under IFRS — financial instruments are measured at fair value/amortised cost using a market rate, with IFRS 15 separately requiring adjustment for any SIGNIFICANT FINANCING COMPONENT within revenue-related receivables — a genuinely converged underlying concept, addressed through somewhat different specific Topics on each side.

Worked Example — Interest Capitalization (Avoidable Interest)

Capitalising financing cost during construction
Example: Constructing a new manufacturing facility
An entity spends an average of $6,000,000 of accumulated expenditures on a qualifying construction project during the year. The entity has $4,000,000 of specific construction-related debt at 6%, and additional general corporate borrowings with a weighted average rate of 8%.
Avoidable interest on the first $4,000,000 (specific debt) = $4,000,000 × 6% = $240,000. Avoidable interest on the remaining $2,000,000 (excess average expenditures, general rate) = $2,000,000 × 8% = $160,000. Total capitalised interest = $240,000 + $160,000 = $400,000, added to the asset's cost rather than expensed as incurred.
8

Worked Example — Imputed Interest on a Non-Interest-Bearing Note

Recording economic substance over stated terms
Example: A non-interest-bearing note received for equipment sold
An entity sells equipment and receives a $500,000 non-interest-bearing note due in 3 years. A market rate of interest for a similar borrower/instrument is determined to be 8%.
Present value of the note = $500,000 ÷ (1.08)³ = approximately $396,900. The note is recorded at $396,900 (not its $500,000 face value), with the $103,100 difference recognised as a DISCOUNT, amortised as INTEREST INCOME over the 3-year term using the effective interest method — reflecting the note's true economic substance rather than its zero-stated interest rate.

Quick-reference glossary preview
Avoidable interest
The amount of interest cost that theoretically could have been avoided if expenditures for a qualifying asset had not been made, the basis for the amount capitalised.
Imputed interest rate
A market rate of interest assigned to a note that does not bear a stated market rate, used to determine the note's present value.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Qualifying asset
An asset that requires a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use, eligible for interest capitalisation.
Capitalization period
The period during which interest cost is capitalised, beginning when expenditures and construction activities have begun and interest cost is being incurred, and ending when the asset is substantially complete and ready for use.
