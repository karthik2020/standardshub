IFRIC 9 – Reassessment of Embedded Derivatives (WITHDRAWN)
Extended reference poster · Withdrawn 2010 · Superseded by IFRS 9 (guidance absorbed directly into the standard) · Retained for historical/comparative reference
🚫 WITHDRAWN INTERPRETATION – SUPERSEDED BY IFRS 9 – IFRIC 9 clarified WHEN an entity must reassess whether an embedded derivative should be separated from its host contract, generally prohibiting reassessment UNLESS there was a change in contract terms significantly modifying cash flows. This guidance was WITHDRAWN and absorbed directly into IFRS 9's own embedded derivative requirements. This poster is retained for historical/comparative reference.

Conceptual Framework Linkage (Historical)

A stability-focused clarification, later absorbed into the standard itself
IFRIC 9 addressed WHEN an entity should REASSESS whether an embedded derivative required separate accounting after INITIAL recognition of the host contract. It concluded that reassessment was GENERALLY PROHIBITED, UNLESS there was a CHANGE in the terms of the contract that SIGNIFICANTLY MODIFIED the cash flows that otherwise would be required – providing STABILITY against continual re-evaluation as market conditions fluctuated.
WHY THIS ABSORPTION HAPPENED
Preventing artificial volatility – WITHOUT this "no reassessment" default rule, entities might have continually reclassified embedded derivatives as market conditions changed, creating INSTABILITY in financial reportingConceptual continuity – when IFRS 9 was developed to replace IAS 39's financial instruments framework entirely, this reassessment logic was considered CORE enough to embed directly within the new standard rather than retain as a cross-referenced interpretation
IFRIC 9 is a genuinely INSTRUCTIVE example, alongside IFRIC 4 and IFRIC 8, of an interpretation whose SUBSTANCE was absorbed almost entirely into its successor standard.
The core reassessment rule IFRIC 9 established
Reassess separation ONLY IF: contract terms change AND that change significantly modifies expected cash flows
ℹ️ This exact "no reassessment absent significant modification" default is preserved within IFRS 9's own embedded derivative framework.
2

Scope & Applicability (Historical)

What IFRIC 9 covered
IFRIC 9 applied to hybrid contracts containing embedded derivatives, addressing the TIMING question of when the separation assessment (originally required under IAS 39) needed to be REVISITED after initial recognition.
Key definitions (historical)
Embedded derivative
A component of a hybrid contract that ALSO includes a non-derivative host contract, with cash flows varying similarly to a standalone derivative.
Significant modification of cash flows
A change in contract terms substantial enough to TRIGGER reassessment under IFRIC 9's exception – a threshold concept preserved in current guidance.
3

The General Prohibition on Reassessment (Historical)

Stability as the default position
The DEFAULT rule was NO reassessment after initial recognition – an entity's ORIGINAL determination of whether an embedded derivative required separation generally STOOD for the life of the contract, preventing continual reclassification as market prices/conditions moved.
4

The Significant Modification Exception (Historical, with worked example)

Historical illustration
Example 1: A loan agreement amendment triggering reassessment
A company held a foreign currency-denominated loan (a hybrid contract, since the loan's principal repayment terms embedded a foreign currency element). At initial recognition, this embedded feature did NOT require separation (closely related to the host). Two years later, the loan was AMENDED, converting the repayment basis to a commodity-price-linked formula UNRELATED to the original host contract terms.
This amendment represented a SIGNIFICANT MODIFICATION of the loan's cash flows, TRIGGERING a fresh reassessment under IFRIC 9 of whether the NEW embedded feature (commodity-price link) required separation from the host – an analysis that would reach the SAME conclusion under IFRS 9's current absorbed reassessment guidance today.
5

Business Combinations & Reassessment (Historical)

A specific carve-out addressed by a later amendment
A LATER amendment to IFRIC 9 (2009) clarified that embedded derivatives in contracts ACQUIRED in a business combination, business combination under common control, or joint venture formation, were EXCLUDED from IFRIC 9's scope at the date of acquisition – this exclusion logic is ALSO preserved within IFRS 9's current framework.
6

Interaction with the Host Contract Classification

Identical to the current approach
IFRIC 9's reassessment logic applied REGARDLESS of the host contract's own classification, focusing SOLELY on whether the embedded derivative's SEPARATION assessment needed revisiting – a distinct question from the host's own measurement basis, a distinction PRESERVED in IFRS 9's current framework (though embedded derivative separation is now LARGELY confined to financial liability hosts and non-financial hosts, given IFRS 9's own classification overhaul for financial ASSET hosts).
7

How This Guidance Became Part of IFRS 9

A genuinely instructive absorption case
When IFRS 9 was developed to REPLACE IAS 39's financial instruments framework in its entirety, the IASB determined IFRIC 9's reassessment logic remained VALID and should be incorporated DIRECTLY into IFRS 9 itself, rather than retained as a separate cross-referenced interpretation. IFRIC 9 was formally WITHDRAWN as IFRS 9 became effective.
8

Common Historical Application Areas

Where this analysis was most frequently applied
Structured notes and convertible bonds with embedded conversion features, commodity-linked supply/purchase contracts, foreign currency-linked loan agreements, and contracts acquired in business combinations – ALL contexts where IFRS 9's current absorbed reassessment guidance remains equally relevant today.
