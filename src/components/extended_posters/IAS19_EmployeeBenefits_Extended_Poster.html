Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why employee benefit obligations are genuine Framework liabilities
When an employee renders service to an entity, a present obligation arises to pay the resulting benefits — whether payable now, or years into the future. IAS 19 exists to ensure the COST of employee service is recognised in the period the service is RENDERED, not merely when cash is eventually paid out.
HOW EMPLOYEE BENEFITS MAP TO THE FRAMEWORK'S ELEMENTS
Present obligation — a legal obligation (contract, legislation) or CONSTRUCTIVE obligation (established practice creating a valid expectation of payment) Past event — the employee's service rendered during the period Expense recognition — the cost of benefits is recognised as the employee earns them, matched to the service period, not the payment date
IAS 19's basic principle is deceptively simple to state — "recognise the cost of benefits when employees earn them, not when paid" — but applying it to long-term, uncertain, post-employment obligations (defined benefit pensions) makes this one of the most actuarially complex standards in IFRS.
Measurement principles applied by IAS 19
Measurement complexity scales directly with the RISK profile of each benefit type — from simple undiscounted amounts to full actuarial present value calculations.
Benefit typeMeasurement basis
Short-term benefitsUndiscounted amount expected to be paid
Defined contribution plansContributions payable for the period (no further measurement needed)
Defined benefit plansPresent value of the obligation, using the Projected Unit Credit method, LESS the fair value of plan assets
Qualitative characteristics in action: the defined benefit obligation's use of actuarial present value and discounting reflects relevance — a long-term pension promise decades from settlement would badly misstate the entity's true obligation if measured undiscounted.

Scope & Applicability

What IAS 19 covers — the four categories
IAS 19 prescribes accounting and disclosure for employee benefits — ALL forms of consideration given by an entity in exchange for service rendered by employees, or for termination of employment.
Short-term employee benefits — wages, salaries, paid annual/sick leave, profit-sharing, bonuses, and non-monetary benefits, all due to be settled WITHIN 12 months of the related service Post-employment benefits — pensions, other retirement benefits, post-employment life insurance/medical care Other long-term employee benefits — long-service/sabbatical leave, jubilee benefits, long-term disability benefits, profit-sharing/bonuses due 12+ months after period end Termination benefits — provided in exchange for the termination of an employee's employment (redundancy)
IAS 19 does NOT cover employee benefits within the scope of IFRS 2 (Share-based Payment) — a critical scope boundary tested frequently in exams.
Key definitions you must know
Defined contribution plans
Post-employment benefit plans under which an entity pays fixed contributions into a separate fund and has NO legal or constructive obligation to pay further contributions if the fund has insufficient assets to pay employee benefits.
Defined benefit plans
Post-employment benefit plans OTHER than defined contribution plans — the entity effectively RETAINS actuarial and investment risk, since it bears the obligation to provide the agreed benefits regardless of fund performance.
Actuarial gains and losses
Changes in the present value of the defined benefit obligation resulting from experience adjustments (differences between previous actuarial assumptions and what actually occurred) and the effects of changes in actuarial assumptions.
Current service cost
The increase in the present value of the defined benefit obligation resulting from employee service in the CURRENT period.

Short-Term Employee Benefits

Recognition & measurement — the simplest category
Recognise the UNDISCOUNTED amount expected to be paid as an expense, unless another Standard requires/permits capitalisation into the cost of an asset (e.g. inventory, PPE)
COMPENSATED ABSENCES — TWO TYPES
Accumulating (e.g. carried-forward annual leave) — recognised as employees render service that increases their entitlement, even if unused Non-accumulating (e.g. sick leave that lapses if unused) — recognised only when the absence actually occurs
Worked example — accrued annual leave
Example 1: Accumulating paid leave provision
At year end, 50 employees have a combined total of 320 unused, carried-forward annual leave days. Average daily pay rate: $250. Leave is accumulating and vests (paid out even on resignation).
Liability for accrued annual leave = 320 days × $250 = $80,000, recognised undiscounted as a short-term employee benefit liability.

Post-Employment Benefits — Defined Contribution Plans

The simple case — contribution equals expense
Because the entity's obligation is limited to the amount it agrees to contribute (with NO further obligation once contributions are paid), accounting is straightforward: the contribution payable for a period is recognised as an EXPENSE, unless another standard requires capitalisation.
Example 2: Defined contribution scheme
An entity contributes 8% of gross salaries to a defined contribution pension scheme. Total gross salaries for the period: $2,400,000. $150,000 of the required contribution remains unpaid at year end.
Total contribution expense = 2,400,000 × 8% = $192,000 (SOPL, in full, regardless of payment timing)
Liability at year end = $150,000 (unpaid portion only)
No actuarial assumptions, discounting, or complex remeasurement is EVER required for defined contribution plans — the entire complexity of Sections 5–7 applies ONLY to defined benefit plans.

Post-Employment Benefits — Defined Benefit Plans

The net defined benefit liability/asset formula
Net defined benefit liability (asset) = Present value of the defined benefit obligation − Fair value of plan assets
THE PROJECTED UNIT CREDIT METHOD
Each period of service gives rise to an ADDITIONAL unit of benefit entitlement, and each unit is measured SEPARATELY to build up the final obligation — requiring actuarial assumptions about mortality, staff turnover, salary growth, and discount rates. This calculation is normally performed by a qualified actuary.
The three components of defined benefit cost
ComponentRecognised in…
Service cost (current + past service cost + gains/losses on settlement)SOPL
Net interest on the net defined benefit liability/assetSOPL
Remeasurements of the net defined benefit liability/assetOCI
This three-way split — the "IAS 19R" model since the 2011 amendment — is the single most important structural fact to memorise about defined benefit accounting.
Worked example — full defined benefit reconciliation
Example 3: Movement in the net defined benefit liability
Opening defined benefit obligation: $4,000,000. Opening plan assets: $3,600,000. Current service cost: $250,000. Discount rate at start of year: 5%. Benefits paid during the year: $180,000. Contributions paid into the plan: $220,000. Actuarial loss on the obligation (change in assumptions): $60,000. Return on plan assets (actual): $200,000.
Net interest on opening net liability = (4,000,000 − 3,600,000) × 5% = 400,000 × 5% = $20,000 (net interest expense, SOPL)

Obligation roll-forward: 4,000,000 + 250,000 (service cost) + 200,000 (interest on obligation: 4,000,000×5%) − 180,000 (benefits paid) + 60,000 (actuarial loss) = $4,330,000 closing obligation
Plan assets roll-forward: 3,600,000 + 180,000 (interest on assets: 3,600,000×5%) − 180,000 (benefits paid) + 220,000 (contributions) + 20,000 (return above expected, remeasurement gain) = $3,840,000 closing assets

Closing net defined benefit liability = 4,330,000 − 3,840,000 = $490,000
Note "return on plan assets" is split: EXPECTED return (at the discount rate) flows through net interest in SOPL; any DIFFERENCE from actual return is a remeasurement in OCI — see Section 6/7.

Remeasurements — Why They Sit in OCI

Components of remeasurement, and the no-recycling rule
Actuarial gains and losses on the defined benefit obligation (experience adjustments and the effects of changes in actuarial assumptions) The difference between actual return on plan assets and the amount included in net interest (i.e. return on plan assets, excluding amounts in net interest) Any change in the effect of the asset ceiling, excluding amounts included in net interest
Remeasurements recognised in OCI are NEVER reclassified ("recycled") to profit or loss in a later period — they may, however, be transferred WITHIN equity (e.g. to retained earnings), similar to a revaluation surplus under IAS 16. This is a frequently tested distinguishing feature versus other OCI items like FX translation reserves (IAS 21), which DO recycle.

Plan Assets, Net Interest & the Asset Ceiling

Net interest mechanics
Net interest = Net defined benefit liability (asset) at the START of the period × Discount rate determined at the START of the period
The SAME discount rate used to measure the obligation is applied to BOTH the obligation and plan assets, ensuring net interest is calculated symmetrically on the NET position.
The asset ceiling
Where plan assets EXCEED the defined benefit obligation (a net surplus/asset position), the recognised asset is capped at the "asset ceiling" — the present value of any economic benefits available in the form of refunds from the plan or reductions in future contributions.
An entity cannot simply recognise a large pension surplus as an asset if it has no realistic way to actually access that surplus (e.g. via refund or contribution holidays) — the asset ceiling prevents overstatement of recoverable pension surpluses.

Other Long-Term Benefits & Termination Benefits

Other long-term employee benefits
Uses a SIMPLIFIED version of defined benefit accounting: the net total of service cost, net interest, AND remeasurements is recognised entirely in PROFIT OR LOSS — unlike defined benefit pensions, there is NO separate OCI presentation for remeasurements here.
Long-service/sabbatical leave Jubilee or other long-service benefits Long-term disability benefits Profit-sharing and bonuses payable 12+ months after period end
Termination benefits — recognition trigger
Recognised at the EARLIER of: when the entity can no longer withdraw the offer of the benefits; and when the entity recognises costs for a restructuring (IAS 37) that involves paying termination benefits.
Termination benefits arise from the DECISION TO TERMINATE, not from employee service rendered — a fundamentally different obligating event from every other category in IAS 19, which is why they're accounted for separately.

Disclosure Requirements

Required disclosures (primarily for defined benefit plans)
CHARACTERISTICS & RISKS
Characteristics of the defined benefit plans and risks associated with them (e.g. asset volatility, discount rate risk, longevity risk) A reconciliation of the opening and closing balances of the defined benefit obligation and plan assets, showing each component of change separately
AMOUNTS & SENSITIVITY
A disaggregation of plan assets by class/nature and risk characteristics Significant actuarial assumptions used (discount rate, salary growth, mortality) Sensitivity analysis for each significant actuarial assumption, showing the effect of reasonably possible changes on the obligation

Critical Judgments & Estimates

Where management judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT ESTIMATES
Discount rate — determined by reference to market yields on high-quality corporate bonds (or government bonds where no deep corporate bond market exists) of matching currency and term Future salary growth rates, mortality/longevity assumptions, staff turnover rates Expected inflation and medical cost trend rates for post-employment medical benefit plans
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Classifying a plan as defined contribution vs defined benefit — sometimes unclear for multi-employer or state plans Whether a constructive obligation exists (e.g. informal practice of enhancing statutory redundancy pay) Applying the asset ceiling test where a plan is in surplus
A small change in the discount rate assumption can have a DISPROPORTIONATELY large effect on the defined benefit obligation for long-duration pension liabilities — this sensitivity is precisely why detailed sensitivity disclosure is mandatory.

IFRS vs US GAAP — Key Differences

Comparison — IAS 19 vs US GAAP (ASC 715)
FeatureIFRS (IAS 19)US GAAP
Actuarial gains/losses (remeasurements)Recognised IMMEDIATELY in OCI, never recycled to SOPLMay be recognised immediately in OCI, OR deferred and amortised using a "corridor" approach; if deferred, EVENTUALLY recycled through SOPL
Past service costRecognised immediately in SOPL, whether vested or unvestedAmortised over the future service period of employees, if unvested
Expected return on plan assetsNo longer a separate concept — net interest uses the SAME discount rate on both obligation and assetsExpected return on assets is separately estimated (potentially different from the discount rate) and included in pension expense
Presentation of pension cost componentsService cost + net interest in SOPL; remeasurements in OCIService cost presented within operating expenses; other components (interest, expected return, amortisation) presented separately, often below operating income
The "corridor approach" permitting delayed, smoothed recognition of actuarial gains/losses under older US GAAP practice is the most significant historical divergence — IFRS's mandatory immediate OCI recognition (since the 2011 IAS 19 amendment) is considerably more conservative and volatility-transparent.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Past service cost
The change in the present value of the defined benefit obligation for employee service in PRIOR periods, resulting from a plan amendment or curtailment — recognised immediately in SOPL.
Curtailment
A significant reduction by the entity in the number of employees covered by a plan, or an amendment reducing future benefits for existing service.
Multi-employer plans
Plans that pool the assets contributed by various entities not under common control, used to provide benefits to employees of more than one entity — classified as DC or DB based on the terms, not simply the pooling structure.
Vested employee benefits
Benefits not conditional on future employment — the entitlement is secured regardless of whether the employee continues to work for the entity.
Plan assets
Assets held by a long-term employee benefit fund, and qualifying insurance policies, that are used solely to settle employee benefit obligations and are not available to the reporting entity's own creditors.
Constructive obligation
An obligation arising from an entity's own established pattern of past practice, published policies, or a sufficiently specific current statement, creating a valid expectation among employees that the entity will discharge those responsibilities.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position (balance sheet) SOPL Statement of profit or loss (income statement) OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
