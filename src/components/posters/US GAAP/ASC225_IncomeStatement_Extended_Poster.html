Conceptual Framework Linkage

Performance over a period, not a point in time
The Income Statement – US GAAP's name for what IFRS calls the Statement of Profit or Loss – reports an entity's revenues, expenses, gains, and losses for a period, arriving at NET INCOME. ASC 225 governs its format and the treatment of items that are unusual, infrequent, or otherwise warrant separate presentation, so that recurring, core operating performance is distinguishable from items less likely to recur.
A 2015 move toward greater IFRS alignment
ASC 225's most significant recent history is the 2015 ELIMINATION of the "extraordinary items" concept (Section 5) – a deliberate simplification that also happened to bring US GAAP into closer alignment with IFRS, which has never permitted extraordinary item presentation.
2

Scope & Applicability

What ASC 225 covers
ASC 225 applies to all entities preparing an Income Statement under US GAAP, addressing the statement's FORMAT, the treatment of unusual or infrequent items, and (via 225-30) the specific recognition of business interruption insurance recoveries.
3

Single-Step vs Multi-Step Format

No single mandated format – but a dominant convention
US GAAP does NOT mandate a single Income Statement format, mirroring IAS 1's own flexibility. In a SINGLE-STEP format, all revenues and gains are grouped together, followed by all expenses and losses, with one subtraction arriving at net income. In a MULTI-STEP format – the dominant convention in US practice, especially among public companies – intermediate subtotals such as GROSS PROFIT and OPERATING INCOME are presented before arriving at net income, giving users a more granular view of performance layers.
4

Nature vs Function Expense Classification

A choice mirroring IAS 1's own
As with IAS 1, entities may classify expenses either by NATURE (e.g. depreciation, employee benefits, raw materials consumed) or by FUNCTION (e.g. cost of goods sold, selling expenses, administrative expenses) – US practice, again, gravitates heavily toward a FUNCTIONAL classification (especially the multi-step format's cost-of-goods-sold-based gross profit calculation), broadly consistent with the market convention IFRS preparers most commonly adopt as well.
5

The Elimination of "Extraordinary Items" (2015)

A concept US GAAP once had that IFRS never did
Before ASU 2015-01 (effective 2016), US GAAP required an item meeting BOTH criteria – UNUSUAL in nature AND INFREQUENT in occurrence – to be presented SEPARATELY as an "extraordinary item," NET OF TAX, below income from continuing operations, with a corresponding earnings-per-share disclosure. ASU 2015-01 ELIMINATED this concept entirely – the FASB concluded the "extraordinary item" test had become vanishingly rare in practice and costly to apply, and dropping it moved US GAAP CLOSER to IFRS, which has never had a comparable concept – IAS 1 explicitly PROHIBITS presenting any items as "extraordinary."
6

Unusual or Infrequent Items – the Residual Disclosure

A lighter-touch replacement requirement
Although "extraordinary items" no longer exist, an item that is unusual in nature OR occurs infrequently (NOT both, as the old test required) is still separately DISCLOSED – but presented as a COMPONENT of income from continuing operations, NOT net of tax, and NOT below a separate "extraordinary" caption. This residual requirement is closer in spirit to IAS 1's general requirement to separately disclose the nature and amount of material items, without a dedicated "unusual items" caption of its own.
7

Discontinued Operations – Cross-Reference to ASC 205-20

Presented separately, governed elsewhere
Results of DISCONTINUED OPERATIONS are presented SEPARATELY, net of tax, below income from continuing operations – but the CLASSIFICATION criteria for what qualifies as a discontinued operation are governed by ASC 205-20 (see the ASC 205 poster), not ASC 225 itself. ASC 225 simply dictates WHERE, on the Income Statement, discontinued operations results must be presented once so classified.
8

Earnings Per Share – Cross-Reference to ASC 260

A required Income Statement disclosure, detailed elsewhere
Public entities (and entities that have filed or are in the process of filing with the SEC) must present BASIC and DILUTED earnings per share on the face of the Income Statement, for income from continuing operations and net income (and separately for discontinued operations, if applicable) – the detailed computation methodology sits in ASC 260, broadly paralleling IAS 33's relationship to IAS 1.
9

Business Interruption Insurance (225-30)

Narrow, US-GAAP-specific guidance
ASC 225-30 provides guidance on the RECOGNITION and CLASSIFICATION of insurance recoveries for business interruption losses – such proceeds are recognised when REALISED or REALISABLE, and are NOT considered extraordinary items (consistent with the elimination in Section 5). There is NO dedicated IFRS standard addressing business interruption insurance recoveries specifically – IFRS preparers would instead apply general principles (IAS 37 for the underlying loss/contingency, IFRS 15/general recognition principles for the insurance recovery itself), making ASC 225-30 a genuinely narrow US-GAAP-specific piece of guidance.

Worked Example – Unusual/Infrequent Item Disclosure

Post-2015 presentation of a large, unusual loss
Example: A one-time regulatory fine
An entity incurs a $6,000,000 regulatory fine following an isolated compliance failure – an event unlikely to recur and unusual relative to the entity's normal operations, but not clearly meeting BOTH the old "unusual AND infrequent" extraordinary-item test given how the specific facts play out.
Under current US GAAP, the $6,000,000 fine is presented as a SEPARATE LINE ITEM WITHIN income from continuing operations (e.g. "Regulatory fine" as its own caption within operating expenses or below operating income), disclosed as unusual in nature – but NOT net of tax, and NOT below a separate "extraordinary items" caption, since that presentation option no longer exists.
11

Worked Example – the Historical Extraordinary Item Test

Illustrating the old (pre-2015) two-part test – for historical/exam reference
Example: A hypothetical earthquake loss under the old rules
Under the PRE-2015 rules, an entity in a region with no history of seismic activity suffers a major, uninsured earthquake loss.
Applying the old two-part test: the loss is UNUSUAL in nature (clearly abnormal and unrelated to ordinary activities) AND INFREQUENT in occurrence (no reasonable expectation of recurrence in the foreseeable future, given no seismic history) – BOTH conditions met, so under the old rules this WOULD have qualified as an "extraordinary item," presented net of tax, below income from continuing operations, with separate EPS disclosure. Under CURRENT US GAAP (post-2015), this same loss is simply presented as an unusual item within continuing operations, per Section 6 – illustrating exactly how the 2015 change altered presentation for what remains, functionally, the same underlying economic event.

ASC 225 vs IAS 1 – Extraordinary Items & Format Flexibility

Comparison – ASC 225 vs IAS 1's Income Statement guidance
FeatureUS GAAP (ASC 225)IFRS (IAS 1)
Extraordinary itemsEliminated in 2015 (ASU 2015-01) – now CONVERGED with IFRS's long-standing prohibitionNever permitted – IAS 1 explicitly prohibits presenting any item as "extraordinary"
Unusual/infrequent item disclosureSpecific residual disclosure requirement in continuing operationsGeneral requirement to separately disclose material items' nature and amount, without a dedicated named category
Format choiceSingle-step / multi-step, free choiceFree choice, similarly unrestricted
Nature vs function expense classificationFree choice, functional dominant in practiceFree choice, also functional-dominant in practice
The 2015 extraordinary-items elimination stands out as one of the clearest examples in the whole Codification of a UNILATERAL US GAAP change made specifically to converge toward existing IFRS practice, rather than a joint FASB-IASB project.
