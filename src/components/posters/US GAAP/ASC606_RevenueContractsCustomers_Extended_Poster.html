Conceptual Framework Linkage

Revenue depicting the transfer of control
ASC 606's core principle: an entity recognises revenue to depict the TRANSFER of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the CONSIDERATION the entity expects to be entitled to in exchange — a genuinely control-based, single unified model replacing the older, more fragmented "risks and rewards"/industry-specific approach that preceded it (see the ASC 605 poster).
The single most converged major standard in this entire library
ASC 606 and IFRS 15 emerged from a genuine JOINT FASB-IASB project, issued SIMULTANEOUSLY in 2014 with substantively IDENTICAL wording and structure. This makes ASC 606 the clearest example anywhere in the Codification of TRUE convergence — the comparison section (20) is correspondingly SHORT, since there is very little to compare.
2

Scope & Applicability

Contracts with customers, broadly
ASC 606 applies to all contracts with CUSTOMERS to transfer goods or services in exchange for consideration — EXCLUDING leases (ASC 842), insurance contracts, financial instruments, guarantees (other than product/service warranties), and certain nonmonetary exchanges between entities in the same line of business to facilitate sales to third parties.

The Five-Step Model — Overview

The complete revenue recognition framework in five steps
StepDescription
1Identify the contract with a customer
2Identify the performance obligations in the contract
3Determine the transaction price
4Allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations
5Recognise revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied
4

Step 1 — Identify the Contract

Five criteria, including collectibility
A contract exists within ASC 606's scope only if: the parties have APPROVED the contract and are committed to their obligations; each party's RIGHTS to goods/services can be identified; PAYMENT terms can be identified; the contract has COMMERCIAL SUBSTANCE; and it is PROBABLE the entity will COLLECT the consideration it is entitled to — this last COLLECTIBILITY criterion is assessed based on the customer's ability and intention to pay, a threshold worth tracking in Section 20's comparison.
5

Step 2 — Identify Performance Obligations

Distinct goods or services
A performance obligation is a promise to transfer a good or service that is DISTINCT — meaning the customer can benefit from it either on its own or with other readily available resources, AND the promise is SEPARATELY IDENTIFIABLE from other promises in the contract (not highly interrelated or significantly customising another item). Bundled arrangements often require careful analysis to determine whether components are genuinely distinct or should be combined into a single obligation.
6

Step 3 — Determine the Transaction Price

The amount the entity expects to be entitled to
The transaction price is the amount of consideration the entity EXPECTS to be entitled to in exchange for transferring goods/services — considering VARIABLE consideration (Section 7), SIGNIFICANT FINANCING COMPONENTS (Section 8), NONCASH consideration (measured at fair value), and consideration PAYABLE to the customer (generally reducing the transaction price unless it's payment for a distinct good/service received from the customer).
7

The Variable Consideration Constraint

Estimate, then constrain
Variable consideration (discounts, rebates, refunds, performance bonuses/penalties) is ESTIMATED using either the EXPECTED VALUE method (probability-weighted sum of possible outcomes, appropriate for a large number of similar contracts) or the MOST LIKELY AMOUNT method (appropriate for a contract with only two possible outcomes) — and then CONSTRAINED: variable consideration is included in the transaction price only to the extent it is PROBABLE that a SIGNIFICANT REVENUE REVERSAL will NOT subsequently occur once the uncertainty resolves.
8

Significant Financing Components

Adjusting for the time value of money
If the TIMING of payments provides the customer or the entity with a SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT of financing (e.g. payment substantially in advance or long after delivery), the transaction price is ADJUSTED for the effects of the time value of money — recognising interest income or expense separately from revenue. A practical expedient allows ignoring this adjustment if the period between transfer and payment is expected to be ONE YEAR OR LESS.
9

Step 4 — Allocate the Transaction Price

Relative standalone selling price
The transaction price is ALLOCATED to each performance obligation based on its RELATIVE STANDALONE SELLING PRICE — the price at which the entity would sell that good/service separately. Where a standalone selling price is not directly observable, it is ESTIMATED (using approaches such as adjusted market assessment, expected cost plus margin, or residual approaches in limited circumstances).
10

Step 5 — Recognise Revenue

Point in time, or over time — a three-part test
Revenue is recognised OVER TIME if ANY ONE of three criteria is met: the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits as the entity performs; the entity's performance creates or enhances an asset the customer controls as it is created; or the asset has no alternative use to the entity AND the entity has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date. If NONE of these criteria are met, revenue is recognised at a POINT IN TIME — when control transfers, assessed using indicators such as physical possession, legal title, risks and rewards of ownership, and customer acceptance.
11

Licensing — Right to Access vs Right to Use

A distinction driving over-time vs point-in-time treatment
A licence of intellectual property provides either a RIGHT TO ACCESS the IP as it exists throughout the licence period (recognised OVER TIME, appropriate where the IP's utility depends on the licensor's ongoing activities that significantly affect it, e.g. a brand actively maintained/developed) or a RIGHT TO USE the IP as it exists at a POINT IN TIME (recognised at that point, appropriate where the IP is more static, e.g. completed software or media content). A specific exception applies sales- or usage-based royalties tied to a licence of IP — recognised only as the underlying sales/usage occurs, not before.
12

Principal vs Agent Considerations

Gross vs net revenue presentation
Where a THIRD PARTY is involved in providing goods/services to a customer, the entity determines whether it is acting as PRINCIPAL (controlling the good/service before transfer to the customer — recognising revenue GROSS, i.e. the full amount charged to the customer) or as AGENT (arranging for another party to provide the good/service — recognising revenue NET, i.e. only its own fee or commission). The key test is whether the entity CONTROLS the specified good/service before it is transferred to the customer.
13

Warranties — Assurance-Type vs Service-Type

Distinguishing a cost accrual from a separate performance obligation
An ASSURANCE-TYPE warranty (simply promising the product will function as specified) is accounted for as a COST ACCRUAL under ASC 460/450, not as a separate revenue performance obligation. A SERVICE-TYPE warranty (providing a service BEYOND assuring the product functions as specified, e.g. extended coverage) IS treated as a SEPARATE performance obligation, with a portion of the transaction price allocated to it and recognised as that additional service is provided.
14

Contract Costs — Cross-Reference to ASC 340-40

Costs to obtain and fulfil a contract
The detailed guidance on capitalising incremental costs to OBTAIN a contract (e.g. sales commissions) and costs to FULFIL a contract lives in ASC 340-40 (covered in the ASC 340 poster) — working directly alongside this Topic's revenue recognition model, and FULLY CONVERGED with IFRS 15's equivalent contract cost guidance given the shared joint-project origin.

Worked Example — the Variable Consideration Constraint

Constraining an estimate subject to a performance bonus
Example: A construction contract with an early-completion bonus
An entity has a $10,000,000 construction contract, with a $500,000 bonus if completed 30 days early. Based on experience with similar contracts, the entity estimates a 60% chance of receiving the bonus (expected value: 60% × $500,000 = $300,000). However, given the project's limited history with this specific site's ground conditions, management concludes it is NOT yet probable a significant revenue reversal would be avoided if the full $300,000 estimate were included now.
Given the genuine uncertainty and the CONSTRAINT test, the entity may recognise a REDUCED estimate (or none at all) of the bonus at contract inception, only including additional amounts as uncertainty resolves throughout the project and confidence in receiving the bonus increases — illustrating how the constraint can produce a lower recognised transaction price than a pure unconstrained expected-value calculation would suggest.
16

Worked Example — Over-Time Recognition

A customised asset with no alternative use
Example: A custom-built machine with a right to payment
An entity builds a highly customised piece of manufacturing equipment for a single customer, with no realistic alternative use if the customer were to cancel, and the contract entitles the entity to payment for costs incurred plus a reasonable margin if the customer terminates without cause at any point.
This meets the THIRD over-time criterion (no alternative use + enforceable right to payment for performance to date) — revenue is recognised OVER TIME, as the entity performs (typically using an input or output method such as costs incurred to date relative to total estimated costs), rather than waiting until final delivery.
17

Worked Example — Principal vs Agent

An online marketplace facilitating third-party sales
Example: A marketplace platform
An online marketplace lists third-party sellers' products, processes customer payments, but never takes title to or physical possession of the goods, and the third-party seller determines pricing and fulfils the order directly. The marketplace charges a 10% commission on each $100 sale.
The marketplace does NOT control the specified good before transfer to the customer — it is acting as an AGENT. It recognises revenue NET, at its $10 commission only — NOT the full $100 gross transaction value, which instead belongs to the third-party seller (the principal).

Quick-Reference Glossary

Contract asset
An entity's right to consideration in exchange for goods/services already transferred, when that right is conditional on something other than the passage of time.
Contract liability
An entity's obligation to transfer goods/services for which it has already received consideration — the modern equivalent of "deferred revenue."
Standalone selling price
The price at which an entity would sell a promised good or service separately to a customer, used to allocate the transaction price.
