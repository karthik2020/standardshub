Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why an intangible is an asset under the Framework
An item qualifies as an asset under the Conceptual Framework if it is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events, with the potential to produce economic benefits — the challenge with intangibles is proving control and separability without physical substance.
HOW INTANGIBLES MAP TO THE FRAMEWORK'S ELEMENTS
Control — the entity has the power to obtain future economic benefits from the resource AND restrict others' access (usually via legal rights such as patents, copyrights, or contracts — without legal enforceability, control is very hard to demonstrate, e.g. for a skilled workforce) Past event — purchase, internal development, or acquisition in a business combination Future economic benefit — revenue from sale of products/services, cost savings, or other benefits from the asset's use
IAS 38 operationalises the Framework's general asset definition for identifiable, non-monetary items without physical substance, deliberately setting a HIGHER recognition bar than for tangible assets because reliable measurement is harder.
Measurement principles applied by IAS 38
The Conceptual Framework lists several possible measurement bases; IAS 38 selects specific ones for initial and subsequent measurement.
Framework basisUsed by IAS 38 for…
Historical costInitial recognition (always)
Current value (fair value)Revaluation model (optional, only if an active market exists)
Value in use / FVLCDRecoverable amount under IAS 36 (impairment)
Qualitative characteristics in action: IAS 38 leans heavily toward faithful representation — this is precisely why internally generated goodwill, brands, and most research costs are banned from recognition: their cost cannot be reliably distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole.
2

Scope & Applicability

What IAS 38 covers
IAS 38 applies to all intangible assets EXCEPT those specifically covered by another standard. An intangible asset is an identifiable, non-monetary asset without physical substance.
THE THREE-PART DEFINITION
(a) Identifiable — separable (can be sold, transferred, licensed, rented, or exchanged) OR arises from contractual/legal rights
(b) Controlled by the entity as a result of past events
(c) Expected to generate future economic benefits
SCOPE EXCLUSIONS — GOVERNED BY OTHER STANDARDS
Intangible assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business → IAS 2 Deferred tax assets → IAS 12 Leases within the scope of IFRS 16 → IFRS 16 Goodwill acquired in a business combination → IFRS 3 (though subsequent impairment testing still follows IAS 36) Financial assets (as defined in IFRS 9) → IFRS 9 Exploration and evaluation assets → IFRS 6
Key definitions you must know
Identifiability
The critical feature separating an intangible asset from goodwill — separable from the entity, or arising from contractual/legal rights, regardless of whether those rights are transferable.
Research
Original and planned investigation undertaken to gain new scientific or technical knowledge and understanding — ALWAYS expensed.
Development
The application of research findings to a plan or design for the production of new/substantially improved materials, devices, products, or processes — capitalised ONLY if strict criteria (PIRATE, see Section 4) are met.
Active market
A market where items traded are homogeneous, willing buyers/sellers can normally be found, and prices are available to the public — rare for intangibles, which is why the revaluation model is seldom used in practice.
Amortisation
The systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an intangible asset with a FINITE useful life over that life — the intangible-asset equivalent of depreciation.

Recognition Criteria

The two-part recognition test — plus the definition hurdle
Before the general recognition test can even be applied, the item must FIRST meet the definition of an intangible asset (identifiable, controlled, future benefits). Only then are the two recognition conditions assessed.
Condition 1 — Probable future economic benefit
It is probable that the expected future economic benefits attributable to the asset will flow to the entity.
Condition 2 — Reliable measurement
The cost of the asset can be measured reliably.
Never recognised as intangible assets, however much is spent: internally generated goodwill, brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists, and similar items generated internally — because they cannot be distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole and lack reliably measurable cost.

Internally Generated Intangibles — Research vs Development

The research/development split
RESEARCH PHASE
Always expensed to SOPL as incurred — no exceptions
DEVELOPMENT PHASE — CAPITALISE ONLY IF ALL "PIRATE" CRITERIA MET
Probable future economic benefits can be demonstrated Intention to complete and use/sell the asset Resources (technical, financial, other) adequate to complete the project Ability to use or sell the intangible asset Technical feasibility of completing the asset Expenditure can be measured reliably
If an entity cannot distinguish the research phase from the development phase of an internal project, ALL expenditure is treated as if incurred in the research phase — i.e. expensed.
Worked example — capitalising development costs
Example 1: Software development project
A company spends $200,000 over a year on a new software product. $80,000 was spent before technical feasibility was established (general feasibility studies) and $120,000 was spent afterward, once management had approved funding and a clear plan to complete and sell the product, with all six PIRATE criteria met from that point onward.
Expensed (research-type, pre-feasibility): $80,000 → SOPL
Capitalised (development, post-feasibility): $120,000 → Intangible asset
Capitalisation begins only from the date all six criteria are FIRST met — never retrospectively for costs already expensed.
JOURNAL ENTRY — DEVELOPMENT COSTS CAPITALISED

SOFP — non-current asset

SOFP
Previously expensed research costs can NEVER be reinstated as an asset in a later period, even if the project later meets all PIRATE criteria.

Subsequent Measurement — Cost vs Revaluation Model

Cost model
Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated amortisation − Accumulated impairment losses
The default and by far the most common model in practice, applied consistently to an entire CLASS of intangible assets.
Revaluation model
Permitted ONLY where fair value can be determined by reference to an ACTIVE MARKET — a condition rarely met for intangibles because most are unique (e.g. patents, brands). Exceptions do exist in practice: taxi licences, fishing quotas, production quotas.
JOURNAL ENTRIES — UPWARD REVALUATION (no prior deficit)

OCIEquity
An intangible asset can ONLY be revalued if it was initially recognised at cost (i.e. it must already qualify for recognition) AND an active market exists — an asset that has never been recognised cannot suddenly be revalued onto the SOFP.
Worked example — indefinite-life asset with no active market
Example 2: Acquired trademark
An entity acquires a well-known trademark for $2m in a separate purchase. Management assesses that there is no foreseeable limit to the period over which the trademark is expected to generate cash inflows, and no active market exists for similar trademarks.
Classification: Indefinite useful life → NOT amortised (see Section 6).
Measurement: Cost model only, since no active market exists for revaluation.
Subsequent treatment: Tested for impairment at least ANNUALLY, and whenever an indicator of impairment exists (IAS 36).
"Indefinite" does not mean "infinite" — it means management can see no foreseeable limit to the period of benefit. The classification is reassessed every period; a change from indefinite to finite is a change in accounting estimate, applied prospectively.
6

Amortisation

Amortisation mechanics by useful life type
FINITE LIFE — STRAIGHT-LINE (DEFAULT)
Annual amortisation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
FINITE USEFUL LIFE
Amortised on a systematic basis over the useful life, reflecting the pattern of consumption of benefits (straight-line used if pattern cannot be determined reliably) Residual value assumed to be ZERO unless a third party commits to purchase it, or an active market exists to determine residual value at end of life Amortisation begins when the asset is available for use, and stops at the earlier of classification as held-for-sale (IFRS 5) or derecognition
INDEFINITE USEFUL LIFE
NOT amortised — instead tested for impairment annually and whenever indicators exist Useful life reassessed each period to confirm indefinite classification remains appropriate
Worked example — patent amortisation
Example 3: Purchased patent
An entity purchases a patent for $600,000 with a legal life of 15 years, but management expects the technology to be commercially viable for only 6 years before it becomes obsolete. Residual value is nil.
Useful life used = 6 years (the SHORTER of legal life and expected economic/commercial life — never automatically the legal life).
Annual amortisation = $600,000 ÷ 6 = $100,000 p.a.

SOFP — asset ↓
Amortisation is normally presented within operating expenses, classified by function or nature, consistent with the entity's policy for other similar expenses.

Impairment Linkage (IAS 36)

How IAS 38 and IAS 36 interact
IAS 38 itself does not contain impairment rules — IAS 36 governs whenever an indicator exists (finite-life assets) or, mandatorily, at least once a year regardless of indicators (indefinite-life assets, goodwill, and intangibles not yet available for use).
MANDATORY ANNUAL IMPAIRMENT TEST — NO TRIGGER NEEDED
Intangible assets with an indefinite useful life Intangible assets not yet available for use (e.g. development projects still in progress) Goodwill acquired in a business combination (tested at the cash-generating unit level)
FINITE-LIFE INTANGIBLES
Tested only when an indicator of impairment exists — same trigger-based approach as PPE under IAS 16.
See the dedicated IAS 36 poster for the full recoverable amount, CGU allocation, and reversal mechanics. Note: impairment losses on goodwill can NEVER be reversed.

Critical Judgments & Estimates

Where management judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT ESTIMATES (IAS 8 — reviewed annually)
Useful life of each finite-life intangible Residual value (usually assumed nil) Amortisation method reflecting the pattern of benefit consumption
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Identifying the point at which technical feasibility is achieved, triggering development cost capitalisation Whether an asset has a finite or indefinite useful life Whether expenditure meets the "identifiability" test (separable or arises from legal/contractual rights) or is simply an internally generated cost that must be expensed
Subsequent expenditure on an already-recognised intangible asset (e.g. ongoing brand advertising, staff training) is almost always expensed — it is very rare that further capitalisation is permitted, because such spend usually cannot be separately attributed to the asset rather than the business as a whole.

IFRS vs US GAAP — Key Differences

Comparison — IAS 38 vs US GAAP (ASC 350 / ASC 730)
FeatureIFRS (IAS 38)US GAAP
Development costsCapitalised once PIRATE criteria metGenerally expensed as incurred (very limited exceptions, e.g. certain software)
Revaluation modelPermitted, only if active market existsNOT permitted — historical cost only
Research costsAlways expensedAlways expensed (same principle)
Impairment test trigger (finite-life)Indicator-based (IAS 36)Indicator-based (similar), but different quantitative test mechanics
Impairment reversalPermitted for finite-life assets (never for goodwill)Prohibited — never reversed
The development-cost treatment is the single most heavily examined IFRS vs US GAAP difference for IAS 38 — under US GAAP, most software and R&D spend hits the income statement immediately, producing materially lower reported intangible assets and higher expenses in the development period.

Related Standards — Cross-Reference Map

How IAS 38 connects across the IFRS body of literature
StandardNature of the connection
IFRS 3Governs goodwill and identifiable intangibles acquired in a business combination — recognised at acquisition-date fair value even if they wouldn't otherwise meet IAS 38's recognition test standalone
IAS 36Provides the impairment testing mechanics (recoverable amount, CGUs, mandatory annual tests, reversal rules) for intangibles and goodwill
IAS 16Shares the recognition/measurement architecture with IAS 38 (cost model, revaluation model, componentisation logic) — the tangible-asset counterpart
IFRS 16Right-of-use assets are excluded from IAS 38's scope even when the underlying asset is intangible in nature (e.g. leased software rights)
IAS 2Intangible assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business (e.g. software developed for resale as inventory) fall under IAS 2, not IAS 38
IFRS 15Costs to fulfil or obtain a contract with a customer may be capitalised under IFRS 15's own criteria, which interact with but are distinct from IAS 38's development criteria
IAS 12Revaluation surpluses on intangibles create taxable temporary differences, generating a deferred tax liability recognised through OCI
IFRS 9Distinguishes intangible assets from financial assets — contractual rights to receive cash are financial assets under IFRS 9, not intangibles under IAS 38
IAS 8Governs how changes in useful life, residual value, and amortisation method are treated (prospective, as changes in estimate)
IAS 1Determines where intangible assets sit on the SOFP (non-current assets) and the general presentation framework the related disclosures sit within
IFRS 5Once classified as held for sale, an intangible asset exits IAS 38's amortisation regime and switches to the lower-of-CA-and-FVLCTS rule

Quick-Reference Glossary

Goodwill
The excess of consideration transferred over the net identifiable assets acquired in a business combination — never recognised internally, only on acquisition (IFRS 3).
Useful life
The period over which an asset is expected to be available for use, or the number of production/similar units expected to be obtained from it — may be finite or indefinite for intangibles.
Separability
The capability of being separated from the entity and sold, transferred, licensed, rented, or exchanged, either individually or with a related contract/asset/liability.
PIRATE criteria
The six mandatory conditions (Probable benefits, Intention, Resources, Ability, Technical feasibility, Expenditure reliably measured) for capitalising development costs.
Recoverable amount
Higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (IAS 36).
Class of intangible assets
A grouping of assets of similar nature/use (e.g. patents, licences, software, brands) used for disclosure and policy application.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position (balance sheet) SOPL Statement of profit or loss (income statement) OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
