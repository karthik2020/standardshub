Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why assets can never be carried above what they can recover
The Conceptual Framework requires assets to be measured such that their carrying amount does not exceed the economic benefits genuinely expected to flow from them. IAS 36 operationalises this by requiring an asset's carrying amount to be tested against its RECOVERABLE AMOUNT — the higher of what it could fetch in a sale, or what it will generate through continued use.
HOW IAS 36 MAPS TO THE FRAMEWORK
Faithful representation — carrying an asset above its recoverable amount would overstate the entity's genuine economic resources, directly violating the Framework's asset measurement objective Prudence — the impairment test is asymmetric by design: assets are written DOWN when recoverable amount falls below carrying amount, but (with the sole exception of certain reversible impairments) are not written back UP purely on optimism Cash-generating units — where an individual asset generates no independent cash flows, the Framework's economic-benefit test is applied at the smallest identifiable GROUP of assets that DOES generate largely independent cash inflows
IAS 36 is the standard that gives PRACTICAL teeth to the Framework's asset definition — an item that no longer meets the "expected to produce economic benefits AT its carrying amount" test must be written down, however it was originally measured under IAS 16, IAS 38, or other cost-based standards.
Measurement principles applied by IAS 36
Recoverable amount blends TWO alternative measurement bases, taking whichever is HIGHER — reflecting that an asset's true economic value is whatever an entity would rationally realise from it, whether through sale or continued use.
Recoverable amount = HIGHER of (Fair value less costs of disposal) and (Value in use)
ℹ️ Qualitative characteristics in action: taking the HIGHER of the two bases reflects relevance — a rational entity would never accept a lower recoverable value than the best of its two genuine options (sell or keep using).
2

Scope & Applicability

What IAS 36 covers
IAS 36 shall be applied in accounting for the impairment of ALL assets, other than those specifically excluded because another Standard already provides its own impairment/measurement mechanism.
SCOPE EXCLUSIONS — GOVERNED BY OTHER STANDARDS
Inventories → IAS 2 (lower of cost and NRV) Contract assets and costs to obtain/fulfil a contract → IFRS 15 Deferred tax assets → IAS 12 Assets arising from employee benefits → IAS 19 Financial assets within IFRS 9's scope → IFRS 9 (expected credit loss model) Investment property measured at fair value → IAS 40 Biological assets measured at fair value less costs to sell → IAS 41 Non-current assets classified as held for sale → IFRS 5
IAS 36 therefore primarily applies to PPE (IAS 16), intangible assets (IAS 38), goodwill (IFRS 3), investment property held at cost, and investments in subsidiaries/associates/joint ventures.
Key definitions you must know
Recoverable amount
The higher of an asset's (or CGU's) fair value less costs of disposal and its value in use.
Value in use
The present value of the future cash flows expected to be derived from an asset or CGU.
Cash-generating unit (CGU)
The smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows that are LARGELY INDEPENDENT of the cash inflows from other assets or groups of assets.
Impairment loss
The amount by which the carrying amount of an asset or CGU exceeds its recoverable amount.

Indicators of Impairment

External indicators
Significant decline in an asset's market value, more than expected from normal use/passage of time Significant adverse changes in the technological, market, economic, or legal environment in which the entity operates Increases in market interest rates likely to affect the discount rate used in value in use, decreasing recoverable amount materially The carrying amount of the entity's net assets exceeds its market capitalisation
Internal indicators
Evidence of obsolescence or physical damage Significant adverse changes in the extent/manner an asset is used, or expected to be used (e.g. plans to discontinue, restructuring, disposal before the previously expected date) Evidence from internal reporting that an asset's economic performance is, or will be, WORSE than expected
⚠️ Mandatory annual test regardless of indicators: goodwill, intangible assets with an indefinite useful life, and intangible assets not yet available for use MUST be tested for impairment at least ANNUALLY — indicators are only the trigger for OTHER assets.

Recoverable Amount — Fair Value Less Costs of Disposal (with worked example)

Fair value less costs of disposal (FVLCD)
FVLCD = Fair value (per IFRS 13) − Incremental costs directly attributable to disposal (e.g. legal costs, transaction taxes, costs of removing the asset) — EXCLUDING finance costs and income tax expense
Example 1: Comparing FVLCD and value in use
A machine has a carrying amount of $600,000. Its fair value in an active market is $520,000, and estimated disposal costs (legal fees, dismantling) are $30,000. Its value in use, based on discounted future cash flows, is calculated at $545,000.
FVLCD = 520,000 − 30,000 = $490,000
Recoverable amount = HIGHER of ($490,000 FVLCD) and ($545,000 value in use) = $545,000
Impairment loss = 600,000 − 545,000 = $55,000
If EITHER FVLCD or value in use exceeds the carrying amount, NO impairment loss is recognised — it is not necessary to calculate BOTH figures if one of them already exceeds the carrying amount.
5

Value in Use — Discounted Cash Flow Mechanics

Estimating future cash flows
Cash flow projections based on REASONABLE and SUPPORTABLE assumptions, giving greater weight to external evidence Based on the most recent financial budgets/forecasts approved by management, covering a maximum period of 5 years (unless a longer period can be justified) Projections beyond the budgeted period extrapolated using a STEADY or DECLINING growth rate, unless an increasing rate can be justified — this rate must NOT exceed the long-term average growth rate for the products/industry/country
⚠️ Cash flow estimates EXCLUDE cash inflows/outflows from financing activities, income tax receipts/payments, and cash flows relating to future RESTRUCTURINGS the entity is not yet committed to, or from future capital expenditure that will improve/enhance the asset's performance beyond its current standard.
The discount rate
A PRE-TAX discount rate reflecting current market assessments of the time value of money AND the risks specific to the asset — for which future cash flow estimates have NOT already been adjusted.
Example 2: Simple value in use calculation
A CGU is expected to generate pre-tax cash flows of $200,000 per year for 5 years, with a pre-tax discount rate of 10%.
Value in use ≈ 200,000 × Annuity factor (10%, 5 years, ≈3.791) = ≈$758,200

Cash-Generating Units & Goodwill (with worked example)

CGU identification and mandatory goodwill allocation
If it is not possible to estimate the recoverable amount of an INDIVIDUAL asset (because it does not generate cash inflows largely independent of other assets), the entity determines the recoverable amount of the CGU to which the asset belongs. Goodwill acquired in a business combination is ALLOCATED, from the acquisition date, to each of the acquirer's CGUs (or groups of CGUs) expected to benefit from the synergies of the combination — even if other assets/liabilities of the acquiree are not assigned to those units.
Example 3: CGU impairment with goodwill
A CGU has a carrying amount of $1,500,000, comprising: goodwill $200,000, PPE $900,000, and other identifiable assets $400,000. The CGU's recoverable amount is determined to be $1,200,000.
Total impairment loss = 1,500,000 − 1,200,000 = $300,000
Allocated FIRST to goodwill in full: $200,000 (fully written off, since it's less than the total impairment)
Remaining $100,000 allocated PRO RATA to other assets in the CGU (see Section 8)
8

Allocating an Impairment Loss Within a CGU

The strict allocation order — with continued worked example
An impairment loss is allocated to reduce the carrying amount of the CGU's assets in this ORDER: FIRST, to any goodwill allocated to the CGU; THEN, to the OTHER assets of the unit PRO RATA on the basis of the carrying amount of each asset — subject to a floor: no individual asset's carrying amount is reduced below the HIGHEST of its own FVLCD (if determinable), its value in use (if determinable), and zero.
Example 3 continued: Allocating the remaining $100,000
Continuing Example 3: after fully writing off the $200,000 goodwill, the remaining $100,000 impairment is allocated pro rata between PPE ($900,000) and other assets ($400,000), based on their relative carrying amounts.
PPE allocation = 100,000 × (900,000 ÷ 1,300,000) ≈ $69,231
Other assets allocation = 100,000 × (400,000 ÷ 1,300,000) ≈ $30,769
If either allocation would reduce an asset below its own recoverable amount (where determinable), the excess is instead reallocated pro rata to the remaining assets.

Reversal of Impairment Losses

Reversal permitted — except for goodwill
An impairment loss recognised in PRIOR periods for an asset OTHER than goodwill is REVERSED if, and only if, there has been a change in the estimates used to determine the asset's recoverable amount since the last impairment loss was recognised.
Reversal capped at: the carrying amount that WOULD have been determined (net of depreciation/amortisation) had NO impairment loss been recognised in prior years
Worked example — reversal of a PPE impairment
Example 4: Impairment reversal capped at original depreciated cost
An asset originally cost $500,000, useful life 10 years. After 3 years (carrying amount $350,000), it was impaired to $210,000. Two years later (5 years total elapsed since purchase), recoverable amount recovers to $340,000. Had no impairment occurred, the carrying amount at year 5 would have been 500,000 × (5/10) = $250,000.
Uncapped potential reversal would suggest restoring to $340,000, but the reversal is CAPPED at $250,000 — the carrying amount that would have existed had no impairment ever occurred (net of the depreciation that would still have been charged).
✕ Goodwill impairment is NEVER reversed, under any circumstances — this is an absolute, permanent prohibition and one of the most fundamental rules in the entire impairment standard.

Disclosure Requirements

Required disclosures
FOR EACH CLASS OF ASSETS
The amount of impairment losses recognised in profit or loss during the period, and the line item(s) in which they're included The amount of reversals of impairment losses recognised in profit or loss during the period Impairment losses/reversals recognised in OCI (for revalued assets)
FOR GOODWILL / INDEFINITE-LIFE INTANGIBLES
Carrying amount of goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles allocated to each CGU (or group of units) Key assumptions used in recoverable amount calculations (e.g. discount rate, growth rate) Sensitivity disclosures where a reasonably possible change in a key assumption would cause the CGU's carrying amount to exceed its recoverable amount

Critical Judgments & Estimates

Where management judgement matters most
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Identifying the boundaries of a CGU — the smallest group generating largely independent cash inflows Whether an internal or external indicator genuinely signals impairment, warranting a full test Whether a change in circumstances since a prior impairment justifies reversal (non-goodwill assets)
SIGNIFICANT ESTIMATES
Future cash flow projections, growth rates, and the pre-tax discount rate used in value in use Fair value less costs of disposal, where no active market price is directly observable Allocation of goodwill and impairment losses across CGUs
⚠️ Impairment testing is one of the most JUDGEMENT-INTENSIVE areas of IFRS — small changes in discount rate or growth rate assumptions can swing a CGU from "no impairment" to a material write-down, making sensitivity disclosure critical for user understanding.

IFRS vs US GAAP — Key Differences

Comparison — IAS 36 vs US GAAP (ASC 350 / ASC 360)
FeatureIFRS (IAS 36)US GAAP
Impairment test for long-lived assets (excl. goodwill)ONE-STEP: compare carrying amount to recoverable amount (higher of FVLCD and VIU) DIRECTLYTWO-STEP: first a recoverability test using UNDISCOUNTED cash flows, THEN measure the loss using fair value if the test fails
Reversal of impairment (non-goodwill)Permitted, subject to the depreciated-historical-cost capPROHIBITED — once impaired, never reversed
Goodwill impairment testing levelReporting unit = CGU or group of CGUsReporting unit concept, broadly similar but with its own specific guidance
Goodwill impairment reversalNEVER reversed — absolute prohibition, convergedNEVER reversed — converged
The TWO most significant differences are: (1) IFRS's single-step recoverable-amount test versus US GAAP's undiscounted-cash-flow screening step for non-goodwill assets, and (2) IFRS's PERMITTING reversal for non-goodwill impairments, which US GAAP prohibits entirely.

Quick-reference glossary
Corporate assets
Assets (e.g. head office building) contributing to multiple CGUs, allocated on a reasonable and consistent basis when testing individual CGUs.
Costs of disposal
Incremental costs directly attributable to disposal of an asset/CGU, excluding finance costs and income tax.
Useful life
The period an asset is expected to be available for use, relevant to projecting cash flows over an appropriate horizon in the value in use calculation.
Financial statement key
SOFP Statement of financial position (balance sheet) SOPL Statement of profit or loss (income statement) OCI Other comprehensive income Equity Statement of changes in equity
