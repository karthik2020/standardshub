SIC-9 – Business Combinations–Classification either as Acquisitions or Unitings of Interests
Historical reference note · Issued 1998 · Withdrawn 2004
⚠ WITHDRAWN – Superseded entirely by IFRS 3 Business Combinations (2004)
What SIC-9 addressed
SIC-9 addressed how to classify a business combination as either an ACQUISITION (one party identifiable as the acquirer, accounted for using the purchase method) or a UNITING OF INTERESTS – also known as "pooling of interests" – a method historically used where no acquirer could be identified, generally involving a genuine mutual sharing of risks and benefits between combining entities, such as a merger of equals.
Why this distinction mattered, and why it disappeared
The pooling-of-interests method avoided recognising goodwill or restating assets to fair value, producing very different (and often more favourable) reported results compared to the purchase/acquisition method. SIC-9 provided criteria to identify the rare genuine "uniting of interests" and distinguish it from an ordinary acquisition. When IFRS 3 was issued in 2004, the IASB ELIMINATED the uniting-of-interests/pooling method ENTIRELY – every business combination within IFRS 3's scope must now use the acquisition method, removing the classification question SIC-9 existed to answer.
Where this guidance lives today
Superseded byIFRS 3 Business Combinations (2004, and subsequent revisions)
Why withdrawnThe pooling-of-interests method SIC-9 helped identify was abolished outright, making the classification question obsolete
Current statusFully superseded – no business combination within IFRS 3's scope may use pooling-of-interests accounting today, regardless of how "merger of equals"-like the transaction appears
