IAS 13 – Presentation of Current Assets and Current Liabilities
Extended reference poster · Historical/withdrawn standard · Origin of the current/non-current split · Mapping to IAS 1
⚠️ WITHDRAWN STANDARD – IAS 13 (issued 1979) established the original principles for classifying and presenting current assets and current liabilities separately from non-current items. It was SUPERSEDED by IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements (1997), which absorbed and substantially refined its classification criteria. For current SOFP presentation requirements, always apply IAS 1.

Conceptual Framework Linkage

Why liquidity classification matters to users
The Conceptual Framework's relevance objective requires users to assess an entity's LIQUIDITY – its ability to meet short-term obligations. IAS 13 established that simply listing assets and liabilities WITHOUT distinguishing those expected to convert to cash (or fall due) within the near term from longer-term items would obscure this critical information – a genuinely foundational classification principle.
HOW IAS 13 MAPPED TO THE FRAMEWORK
Relevance – separating current from non-current items directly serves users assessing short-term liquidity and working capital adequacy Understandability – a clear, consistent classification convention (rather than an undifferentiated list) makes the SOFP far easier for users to interpret The operating cycle as the classification anchor – IAS 13 recognised that a rigid 12-month cutoff would misclassify items for businesses with LONGER natural operating cycles (e.g. long-term construction, wine production), introducing the OPERATING CYCLE concept as an alternative benchmark
IAS 13's operating cycle concept – allowing the current/non-current cutoff to extend BEYOND 12 months for businesses with a longer natural cycle – survives essentially UNCHANGED as a core feature of IAS 1's own current/non-current classification test today.
The classification test, then and now
ConceptStatus
12-month OR operating cycle testEstablished by IAS 13; UNCHANGED today
Mandatory current/non-current presentationEncouraged by IAS 13; MANDATORY (with narrow exception) under IAS 1

Scope & Applicability (Historical)

What IAS 13 covered
IAS 13 applied to the presentation of current assets and current liabilities in financial statements, establishing definitions and classification principles applicable across all entities.
This general applicability, without industry carve-outs, is retained identically in IAS 1's own current/non-current guidance today.
Key definitions you must know (historical)
Current asset (original)
Cash, or an asset held primarily for the purpose of being traded, or expected to be realised, sold, or consumed within the normal operating cycle, or within 12 months.
Operating cycle (original)
The time between the acquisition of materials entering a process and their realisation in cash or cash equivalents.
3

The Original Current/Non-Current Test (with worked example)

Whichever is LONGER – 12 months, or the operating cycle
IAS 13 classified an asset as CURRENT if it was expected to be realised, or was held for sale/consumption, in the normal course of the entity's operating cycle, OR within 12 months of the reporting date – WHICHEVER PERIOD WAS LONGER.
Example 1: A wine producer's extended operating cycle
A winery's normal operating cycle – from grape harvest through fermentation, ageing, and eventual sale – takes approximately 30 months. At the reporting date, the winery holds wine inventory expected to be sold in 24 months.
Even though the 24-month expected sale period EXCEEDS the standard 12-month benchmark, the inventory is STILL classified as a CURRENT asset – because it falls WITHIN the winery's normal 30-month OPERATING CYCLE, which is the relevant (longer) benchmark in this case.
4

The Operating Cycle Concept

Recognising that "12 months" doesn't fit every business
IAS 13 recognised that a RIGID 12-month rule would misclassify inventory and related working capital items for industries with naturally LONGER production/sale cycles (shipbuilding, aged spirits, long-term construction). Where the operating cycle could NOT be clearly identified, a default assumption of 12 months was used instead – a fallback still applied identically under IAS 1 today.
5

Working Capital Presentation

Encouraging (though not universally mandating) a working capital subtotal
IAS 13 encouraged presentation of a WORKING CAPITAL subtotal (current assets less current liabilities) on the face of the SOFP, viewing this as a genuinely USEFUL liquidity indicator for users – a presentation convention many entities STILL follow today, even though IAS 1 does not mandate this specific subtotal in all circumstances.
6

Classification of Specific Items

Early guidance on borderline cases
Cash restricted for a purpose beyond 12 months was classified as NON-current, an early recognition that legal/contractual restrictions could override a simple "it's cash" presumption Trade receivables/payables due beyond 12 months, but within the normal operating cycle, remained CURRENT The current portion of long-term debt (principal due within 12 months) was classified as a CURRENT liability, even though the debt instrument as a whole was long-term in nature
Each of these early classification principles survives essentially UNCHANGED as specific guidance within IAS 1 today.
7

Offsetting Current Items

A general presumption against netting
IAS 13 established that current assets and current liabilities should generally NOT be offset against each other, UNLESS a legal right of set-off existed and the entity intended to settle net – a principle carried forward IDENTICALLY into IAS 1's own general offsetting prohibition today.
8

Why IAS 13 Was Absorbed into IAS 1

Consolidated into a comprehensive presentation standard
As IAS 1 developed into a comprehensive standard governing the ENTIRE structure and presentation of financial statements, it made sense to absorb IAS 13's current/non-current classification guidance directly, rather than maintaining it as a SEPARATE, narrower standard – IAS 13 was withdrawn upon IAS 1's original 1997 issuance.

Critical Judgments & Estimates (Historical)

Where management judgement mattered most
SIGNIFICANT JUDGEMENTS
Determining the entity's normal operating cycle, where it extended beyond 12 months Classifying borderline items (restricted cash, long-term receivables due within the operating cycle)
SIGNIFICANT ESTIMATES
Expected realisation/settlement timing for specific current assets/liabilities
⚠️ Determining the "normal operating cycle" for industries WITHOUT a clearly defined production process (e.g. service businesses) was, even then, a genuinely difficult judgement – a limitation that persists identically under IAS 1 today.
