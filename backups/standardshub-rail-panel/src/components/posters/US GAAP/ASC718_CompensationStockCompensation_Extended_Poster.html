Conceptual Framework Linkage

Equity awards are a genuine cost of service, not a free lunch
Stock options and other equity awards granted to employees are a genuine form of COMPENSATION — the entity is paying for services with equity instruments instead of (or alongside) cash. ASC 718 exists to ensure this cost is recognised as an EXPENSE over the period the service is rendered, at FAIR VALUE, rather than the (now fully abandoned) old "intrinsic value" approach that often produced little or no recognised expense for at-the-money option grants.
Converged on the big picture, diverging on some real mechanics
ASC 718 and IFRS 2 share the SAME fundamental fair-value-based philosophy — but differ on graded vesting attribution flexibility (Section 6), the forfeiture policy election (Section 7), and excess tax benefit treatment (Section 9), each worth knowing well.
2

Scope & Applicability

Employee and non-employee share-based payments
ASC 718 applies to share-based payment awards granted to EMPLOYEES, and — since ASU 2018-07 extended this Topic's measurement principles to non-employees too — awards to consultants, vendors, and other non-employees as well, unifying what had previously been a separate measurement model (ASC 505-50) under one framework.
3

Fair Value Measurement — Equity-Classified Awards

Grant-date fair value, fixed regardless of later stock price moves
EQUITY-classified awards (most stock options, RSUs settled in shares) are measured at their GRANT-DATE fair value — using an option-pricing model (Black-Scholes, a lattice/binomial model, or Monte Carlo simulation for more complex awards) for options, or simply the quoted share price for full-value awards like RSUs. This grant-date value is NEVER subsequently remeasured, regardless of how the entity's stock price later moves — the SAME grant-date fair value approach IFRS 2 requires for equity-settled awards, a converged core principle.
4

Liability-Classified Awards — Remeasured Each Period

Also converged
LIABILITY-classified awards (e.g. cash-settled stock appreciation rights, or awards with put features requiring cash settlement) are measured at fair value at EACH reporting date, with changes recognised in compensation expense until settlement — the SAME remeasurement approach IFRS 2 requires for cash-settled awards.
5

Expense Recognition Over the Requisite Service Period

Matched to the vesting/service period
Compensation cost is recognised over the REQUISITE SERVICE PERIOD — generally the vesting period — reflecting that the award is compensation for services rendered DURING that period, not a one-time grant-date event.
6

Graded Vesting — Straight-Line vs Graded Attribution

A genuine flexibility not available under IFRS 2
For AWARDS WITH GRADED VESTING (e.g. 25% vesting each year over 4 years), US GAAP permits an ACCOUNTING POLICY ELECTION between (a) STRAIGHT-LINE attribution — recognising the TOTAL award's expense evenly over the entire vesting period, or (b) GRADED (ACCELERATED) attribution — treating each vesting tranche as its OWN separate award with its OWN vesting period, front-loading more expense into earlier periods. IFRS 2 does NOT offer this choice — it effectively REQUIRES the graded/accelerated attribution method (treating each tranche as a separate grant), since IFRS 2 requires each tranche to be recognised based on its OWN specific vesting date. This genuine flexibility (a policy CHOICE under US GAAP vs a MANDATED single method under IFRS) is a real, practically significant divergence.
7

Forfeitures — the ASU 2016-09 Policy Election

A 2016 US-specific simplification, adding a second election IFRS 2 doesn't offer
ASU 2016-09 introduced a POLICY ELECTION: an entity may EITHER continue ESTIMATING forfeitures expected to occur (the historical, and still IFRS 2-required, approach), OR elect to account for forfeitures AS THEY ACTUALLY OCCUR (a simplification, reversing previously recognised expense only when an employee actually forfeits, rather than building in an ongoing forfeiture estimate). IFRS 2 has NO equivalent "as they occur" alternative — it generally requires ESTIMATING forfeitures throughout, with no election to instead true-up only upon actual forfeiture. This is a second, genuinely US-specific flexibility point layered on top of the graded vesting choice in Section 6.
8

Modifications — the Incremental Fair Value Approach

Broadly converged
When an award's terms are MODIFIED, the entity compares the award's fair value IMMEDIATELY BEFORE and IMMEDIATELY AFTER the modification, recognising any INCREMENTAL fair value as additional compensation cost (over the remaining vesting period, or immediately if already vested) — the same general incremental fair value concept IFRS 2 applies to modifications.
9

Income Tax Effects — the ASU 2016-09 Simplification

A significant 2016 procedural change
Before ASU 2016-09, EXCESS TAX BENEFITS (or deficiencies) — arising when an award's actual tax deduction differs from its book compensation cost — were recognised in ADDITIONAL PAID-IN CAPITAL (APIC), NOT the income statement. ASU 2016-09 CHANGED this: excess tax benefits/deficiencies are now recognised DIRECTLY in the INCOME STATEMENT, as a component of income tax expense/benefit — a significant simplification eliminating the need to track a notional "APIC pool." This is primarily a US-GAAP-INTERNAL procedural simplification; IFRS's own tax treatment under IAS 12 has some differing specific mechanics (particularly around excess deductions tied to intrinsic value at exercise versus grant-date fair value), though broadly similar income-statement-based treatment applies in many jurisdictions today.

Worked Example — Basic Grant-Date Fair Value Expense

Straight-line expense over a simple cliff-vesting grant
Example: A 3-year cliff-vesting option grant
An entity grants stock options with a grant-date fair value of $1,200,000, cliff-vesting after 3 years of continued service (no graded tranches).
Annual compensation expense = $1,200,000 ÷ 3 = $400,000 per year for 3 years: Debit Compensation expense $400,000 / Credit APIC — stock options $400,000, each year, regardless of subsequent stock price movement.
11

Worked Example — Graded Vesting Attribution

Straight-line vs graded attribution for the same award
Example: A 4-year, 25%-per-year graded vesting grant
An entity grants options with a total grant-date fair value of $2,000,000, vesting 25% per year over 4 years.
Straight-line election: $2,000,000 ÷ 4 = $500,000 recognised EVENLY each year.
Graded (accelerated) attribution (mandatory under IFRS 2, elective under US GAAP): treating each 25% tranche as its own separate award, MORE expense is recognised in EARLIER years than later ones — a front-loaded expense pattern. The SAME total $2,000,000 award produces a materially DIFFERENT year-by-year expense profile depending on which method is elected, even though the cumulative total over 4 years is identical either way.
12

Worked Example — the Forfeiture Policy Election

Estimating forfeitures vs recognising them as they occur
Example: A grant with an expected 5% forfeiture rate
An entity grants $1,000,000 of grant-date fair value in options, cliff-vesting in 4 years, with an estimated 5% forfeiture rate based on historical turnover.
Estimate forfeitures election (required under IFRS 2): annual expense = ($1,000,000 × 95%) ÷ 4 = $237,500/year, building in the expected forfeiture from the start.
Account for forfeitures as they occur election (available only under US GAAP): annual expense = $1,000,000 ÷ 4 = $250,000/year, with NO forfeiture estimate built in — expense is only REVERSED in the specific period an employee actually forfeits their award.

ForfeituresPOLICY CHOICE — estimate, or recognise as incurredMust ESTIMATE forfeitures; no "as incurred" alternative

The two policy ELECTIONS available under US GAAP but not IFRS 2 (attribution method and forfeiture treatment) are the most practically significant divergence points — they can produce genuinely different period-by-period expense patterns for the identical underlying award.

Requisite service period
The period over which an employee must provide service to earn the right to a share-based award, generally the vesting period.
Excess tax benefit
The tax benefit realised in excess of the tax benefit previously recognised based on grant-date fair value, now recognised in the income statement.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Graded vesting
An award vesting in tranches over time (e.g. 25% per year for 4 years), rather than all at once.
Incremental fair value
The excess of an award's fair value immediately after a modification over its fair value immediately before, recognised as additional compensation cost.
