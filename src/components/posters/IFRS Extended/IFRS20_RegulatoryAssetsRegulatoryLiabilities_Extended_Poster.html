Conceptual Framework Linkage

Closing a genuine, long-standing IFRS gap
IFRS 20 fills a gap IFRS has lacked COMPREHENSIVE guidance on: when a company is subject to RATE REGULATION (a regulator determines how much and WHEN it can charge customers), a mismatch can arise between when goods/services are SUPPLIED and when the related COMPENSATION is actually charged to customers under the regulatory agreement — IFRS 15 revenue alone may NOT fully reflect the company's performance for the period. IFRS 20 requires recognising the TOTAL ALLOWED COMPENSATION for regulatory goods/services in the SAME period they are supplied, using regulatory assets/liabilities to bridge any timing gap.
HOW IFRS 20 MAPS TO THE FRAMEWORK
Faithful representation — aligning recognised income with the ECONOMIC entitlement the regulatory agreement creates, rather than only the cash-timing-driven IFRS 15 revenue figureComparability — a SINGLE comprehensive model REDUCES the diversity in practice that previously existed across rate-regulated companies globally, since IFRS had no dedicated standard (only IFRS 14's narrow, OPTIONAL transitional relief) before now
IFRS 20 is NOT sector-specific and, unlike IFRS 14, is NOT optional — any company meeting its scope criteria MUST apply it, a significant difference from its narrow, elective predecessor.
The core principle — identical across the standard
Recognise TOTAL ALLOWED COMPENSATION for regulatory goods/services in the SAME period they are supplied — bridging any timing gap via regulatory assets/liabilities
ℹ️ This is an "OVERLAY" onto existing standards — IFRS 15 revenue is recognised FIRST, then IFRS 20 regulatory income/expense adjusts the total to match allowed compensation.
2

Scope & Applicability

What IFRS 20 covers
IFRS 20 applies where (1) the company and a regulator are party to an agreement PRESCRIBING the regulated rate the company can charge customers, and (2) part of the total allowed compensation for goods/services delivered in ONE period is charged to customers in a DIFFERENT period — creating timing differences. Effective 1 January 2029, MANDATORY (not optional) for entities meeting scope criteria, superseding IFRS 14.
Key definitions you must know
Difference in timing
Part or all of the total allowed compensation for regulatory goods/services supplied in ONE period is included in regulated rates charged to customers in a DIFFERENT period.
Total allowed compensation
The total amount the company is ENTITLED to earn for regulatory goods/services supplied in a period, per the regulatory agreement — the standard's central benchmark concept.
3

Differences in Timing — The Core Concept (with worked example)

The engine driving regulatory asset/liability recognition
Example 1: An under-recovery creating a regulatory asset
A power distribution utility incurs $50 million of ELIGIBLE costs during the year that its regulatory agreement allows it to recover, but the applicable tariff formula only permits it to charge customers $35 million in the CURRENT year, with the remaining $15 million recoverable through HIGHER tariffs in future years.
The $15 million shortfall represents a DIFFERENCE IN TIMING — the company has an ENFORCEABLE present right to add this amount to future regulated rates, so it recognises a REGULATORY ASSET of $15 million (discounted at the regulatory interest rate) and corresponding REGULATORY INCOME, ensuring TOTAL income recognised for the period ($35 million IFRS 15 revenue + $15 million regulatory income = $50 million) matches the TOTAL allowed compensation actually earned.
4

Total Allowed Compensation

The standard's central benchmark
IFRS 20 provides guidance on the MOST COMMON types of compensation/deductions specified in regulatory agreements (e.g. recovery of specific costs, a return on a regulatory capital base, performance incentives/penalties) to help entities correctly identify what the total allowed compensation for a period actually comprises.
5

Recognition of Regulatory Assets & Liabilities

Enforceable rights and obligations, all recognised
A REGULATORY ASSET is recognised when the entity has an ENFORCEABLE present right to ADD an amount to future regulated rates; a REGULATORY LIABILITY when it has an enforceable present OBLIGATION to DEDUCT an amount from future regulated rates. IFRS 20 requires recognition of ALL regulatory assets/liabilities existing at the reporting date, subject to a SPECIFIC recognition criterion for those arising from regulatory depreciation of a regulatory capital base, and a SIMPLIFIED measurement approach for compensation based on unobservable-input benchmarks.
6

Measurement — The Cash-Flow-Based Technique

A discounted cash flow approach, regulatory-rate-anchored
Regulatory assets/liabilities are MEASURED using a cash-flow-based technique — estimating the FUTURE cash flows arising from recovery of a regulatory asset (or fulfilment of a regulatory liability), DISCOUNTED using the REGULATORY INTEREST RATE specified in the regulatory agreement for that specific balance, rather than a general market discount rate.
7

The "Overlay" Approach — How IFRS 20 Works with IFRS 15

A genuinely distinctive structural feature
IFRS 20 does NOT replace IFRS 15 revenue recognition — it operates as an "OVERLAY." A company FIRST applies IFRS 15 (and other relevant standards) NORMALLY, then LAYERS ON TOP any regulatory income/expense arising from differences in timing, adjusting the total reported income/expense to match the total allowed compensation actually earned in the period. Recognition of a regulatory asset/liability has NO impact on cash flows — it is a purely accrual-based bridging mechanism.

Sectors Most Affected

Where IFRS 20 will have the greatest practical impact
IFRS 20 mainly affects entities in industries such as ELECTRICITY, GAS, WATER, and TRANSPORTATION that are subject to rate regulation — sectors where tariff true-up mechanisms and cost recovery timing differences from regulators are an established operational feature. The IASB's own Effects Analysis notes companies in these sectors should expect changes to key financial metrics such as EBITDA, working capital, and return on assets once IFRS 20 is adopted.
