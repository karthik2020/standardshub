Conceptual Framework Linkage

A supplementary standard, continuing existing practice rather than prescribing a new model
FRS 103 sits ALONGSIDE FRS 102 (or FRS 101), supplementing the general framework for entities that ISSUE insurance contracts. Rather than PRESCRIBING a comprehensive new measurement model, FRS 103 largely permits INSURERS to CONTINUE their EXISTING accounting practices for insurance liabilities — an approach directly INHERITED from the OLD international standard IFRS 4, which itself was always intended as a TEMPORARY, interim solution pending a more comprehensive international project (which has SINCE concluded internationally with IFRS 17, but which FRS 103 has NOT adopted an equivalent of — Section 6).
2

Scope

Entities issuing insurance contracts
FRS 103 applies to entities that ISSUE insurance contracts (transferring significant insurance risk from a policyholder to the insurer) or REINSURANCE contracts, supplementing whichever primary standard (FRS 102 or FRS 101) otherwise applies to the entity.
3

Continuation of Existing Insurance Accounting Practices

A genuine "grandfather your practice" relief, much like IFRS 4 historically was
FRS 103 largely permits an insurer to CONTINUE applying its EXISTING accounting policies for insurance CONTRACTS (measurement of insurance liabilities, recognition of acquisition costs, and so on) — SUBJECT to certain baseline improvements (e.g. the liability adequacy test, Section 4) and PROHIBITIONS on introducing NEW practices that would move FURTHER away from general accounting principles. This "continue existing practice" approach closely MIRRORS how the OLD international standard IFRS 4 operated before being SUPERSEDED internationally by IFRS 17.
4

The Liability Adequacy Test

A minimum check that recognised liabilities are sufficient
At EACH reporting date, an insurer must assess whether its RECOGNISED insurance liabilities are ADEQUATE, using CURRENT estimates of future cash flows under its insurance contracts. If the assessment shows the CARRYING amount is INADEQUATE, the ENTIRE deficiency is recognised IMMEDIATELY in the Profit and Loss Account — a baseline MINIMUM adequacy check layered on top of whatever EXISTING measurement policy the insurer otherwise applies.
5

Insurance Contract Classification — Significant Insurance Risk

A contract must transfer SIGNIFICANT insurance risk to qualify
A contract is classified as an "INSURANCE CONTRACT" under FRS 103 only if it transfers SIGNIFICANT insurance risk — broadly, the possibility that the insurer will need to pay SIGNIFICANTLY MORE in a scenario where an insured event occurs than it would otherwise. Contracts that do NOT transfer significant insurance risk (e.g. purely investment-linked contracts with no meaningful insurance element) are instead accounted for as FINANCIAL INSTRUMENTS under the general FRS 102 (Sections 11/12) framework.
6

THE KEY DIVERGENCE — FRS 103 Is Based on the Superseded IFRS 4, Not IFRS 17

A second-order divergence — FRS 103 hasn't followed IFRS's own subsequent overhaul
Internationally, IFRS 4 (the standard FRS 103 was originally modelled on) has been SUPERSEDED by IFRS 17 — a WHOLESALE replacement introducing a fundamentally NEW insurance measurement architecture (fulfillment cash flows PLUS a contractual service margin representing unearned profit, released over the coverage period — see the companion ASC 944 poster for full mechanics). FRS 103 has NOT been updated to incorporate an equivalent of IFRS 17 — UK and Irish insurers reporting under FRS 102/103 CONTINUE to apply FRS 103's OLDER, IFRS-4-STYLE "continue existing practice" approach, UNLESS they separately elect to apply FULL IFRS (which WOULD then require IFRS 17). This is a genuinely NOTABLE, well-recognised divergence — NOT merely between UK GAAP and IFRS in general, but a case where UK GAAP's insurance standard has NOT kept pace with IFRS's OWN subsequent, comprehensive reform of the SAME topic area — mirroring, at the UK-GAAP-vs-IFRS level, a similar structural gap to the one between ASC 944 (US GAAP, largely traditional model with LDTI improvements) and IFRS 17 discussed in the US GAAP poster series.

Worked Example — Insurance Risk Classification

Distinguishing genuine insurance contracts from investment contracts
Example: A term life policy vs a pure investment bond
An insurer issues (a) a term life insurance policy paying a lump sum significantly greater than the accumulated premiums if the policyholder dies during the term, and (b) an investment bond with no life cover element, simply tracking an underlying fund's value.
Policy (a) transfers SIGNIFICANT insurance risk (a materially larger payout on death) and is classified as an INSURANCE CONTRACT under FRS 103. Policy (b), having NO meaningful insurance risk element, is classified as a FINANCIAL INSTRUMENT, accounted for under FRS 102's general financial instruments sections instead.
8

Worked Example — the Liability Adequacy Test

Topping up an inadequate reserve
Example: A general insurer's claims reserve
An insurer's recognised insurance liability for a book of motor policies is £40,000,000. A current estimate of future cash flows under these contracts indicates the liability should be £43,500,000.
Since the CURRENT estimate (£43,500,000) EXCEEDS the recognised carrying amount (£40,000,000), the insurer recognises the FULL £3,500,000 DEFICIENCY immediately in the Profit and Loss Account — topping up the recognised liability to reflect the adequacy test's result.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Significant insurance risk
The transfer of meaningfully greater potential payout risk that qualifies a contract as an insurance contract under FRS 103.
Liability adequacy test
A minimum assessment, at each reporting date, of whether recognised insurance liabilities are sufficient based on current cash flow estimates.
