Conceptual Framework Linkage

Classification drives measurement, drives where gains/losses land
How a debt security is CLASSIFIED determines whether it is measured at amortised cost or fair value, and — if fair value — whether changes flow through net income or OCI. ASC 320 exists to define these classification categories precisely, since classification (not measurement mechanics per se) is where most of the real judgement, and most divergence from IFRS, actually lives.
A Topic reshaped by ASU 2016-01
ASC 320's TITLE still references both debt and equity securities, but ASU 2016-01 (effective 2018) moved essentially ALL equity security guidance into the newly created ASC 321, leaving ASC 320 to govern primarily DEBT securities today — a naming legacy worth knowing, since the Topic's scope no longer matches its full title.
2

Scope — Now Primarily Debt Securities

What remains within ASC 320 today
ASC 320 governs investments in DEBT securities — instruments representing a creditor relationship with the issuer, such as corporate bonds, government securities, and mortgage-backed securities — classified into one of three categories (Section 3). Equity securities (representing an ownership interest) are addressed separately under ASC 321.

4

Held-to-Maturity — the Positive Intent and Ability Test

A strict, intent-based classification test
HTM classification requires the entity to have BOTH the POSITIVE INTENT and the ABILITY to hold the security to MATURITY — a strict test. Certain SALES do not automatically "taint" the entire HTM portfolio, including sales close to maturity (when interest rate risk is no longer a meaningful factor in fair value), sales after collecting substantially all of the original principal, and sales attributable to specific, isolated, non-recurring circumstances beyond the entity's control.
5

Trading Securities

Held principally for near-term resale
TRADING securities are bought and held principally for the purpose of SELLING them in the NEAR TERM — typically reflecting active and frequent buying and selling, generating profits on short-term price movements. Both realised and UNREALISED gains and losses are recognised in NET INCOME as they occur.
6

Available-for-Sale Securities

The residual, default category
Debt securities NOT classified as HTM or trading are classified as AVAILABLE-FOR-SALE (AFS) — measured at fair value, with UNREALISED gains and losses recognised in OTHER COMPREHENSIVE INCOME. Upon SALE, the CUMULATIVE gain or loss previously recognised in OCI is RECLASSIFIED ("recycled") into net income — a mechanic notably UNAVAILABLE for equity securities under ASC 321, which have no OCI category at all (see the ASC 220 and ASC 321 posters).
7

Transfers Between Categories

Permitted only in rare circumstances
Transfers of debt securities BETWEEN the three categories are permitted, but transfers INTO or OUT OF the trading category are expected to be RARE, given the near-term-resale intent trading classification implies. Transfers between HTM and AFS also carry specific rules — for example, a transfer OUT of HTM raises questions about whether the entity's positive intent/ability assertion remains valid for its REMAINING HTM portfolio.
8

Credit Losses on AFS Debt Securities — Cross-Reference to ASC 326

An allowance approach, replacing the old OTTI model
Credit losses on AFS debt securities are now measured under ASC 326-30, using an ALLOWANCE approach (a contra-asset, credit loss allowance account) rather than the pre-2016 "other-than-temporary impairment" (OTTI) model, which required DIRECT write-downs of the security's cost basis. The allowance approach permits RECOVERY of previously recognised credit losses through a REVERSAL of the allowance if credit conditions improve — a meaningful change from the old OTTI model, which generally did not permit such reversal once a direct write-down occurred.

Worked Example — AFS Debt Security Fair Value Movement

Tracking an unrealised gain through OCI, then recycling on sale
Example: Purchase, remeasurement, and sale of an AFS bond
An entity purchases a corporate bond for $1,000,000, classified AFS. At the next reporting date, its fair value has risen to $1,050,000. The entity later sells the bond for $1,080,000.
At remeasurement: Debit AFS security $50,000 / Credit OCI $50,000 (unrealised gain, no net income impact).
At sale: Debit Cash $1,080,000 / Credit AFS security $1,050,000 / Credit Realised gain (net income) $30,000 (the further gain since the last remeasurement) — AND the entity reclassifies the previously deferred $50,000 OCI gain into net income as well, per the recycling mechanic, so total net income recognised on sale reflects the FULL $80,000 gain from original cost.
10

Worked Example — HTM Classification Test

Assessing positive intent and ability
Example: A bond purchased for a liquidity buffer
An entity purchases a 10-year government bond, intending and having sufficient liquidity/funding stability to hold it to maturity, with no anticipated need to sell it in response to changes in interest rates, liquidity needs, or other factors.
This QUALIFIES for HTM classification — the entity has demonstrated BOTH positive intent (genuine plan to hold to maturity) AND ability (sufficient financial resources and flexibility that it would not be forced to sell). The bond is measured at AMORTISED COST, with no fair value remeasurement through either net income or OCI.

ASC 320 vs IFRS 9 — Intent-Based vs Business-Model Classification

Comparison — ASC 320 vs IFRS 9's debt instrument classification
FeatureUS GAAP (ASC 320)IFRS 9
Classification basisManagement's stated INTENT for each individual security (HTM/trading/AFS)The entity's overall BUSINESS MODEL for managing a group of assets, combined with the CASH FLOW CHARACTERISTICS (SPPI test) of the specific instrument
Number of categoriesThree: HTM, trading, AFSThree (broadly analogous): amortised cost, FVOCI, FVTPL
Reclassification frequencyRare, especially for HTM/tradingReclassification permitted only when the BUSINESS MODEL itself changes — also expected to be rare
"Tainting" consequences of early salesCan call the ENTIRE HTM portfolio's classification into questionIFRS 9's business-model test is somewhat more resilient to occasional sales, since it looks at the OVERALL objective of the business model rather than the intent for each individual instrument
Both frameworks reach broadly similar THREE-CATEGORY outcomes, but via genuinely different underlying LOGIC — an instrument-by-instrument intent test under US GAAP, versus a portfolio-level business-model-plus-cash-flow-characteristics test under IFRS 9 — meaning the classification of an economically identical security can, in edge cases, differ between the two frameworks.
