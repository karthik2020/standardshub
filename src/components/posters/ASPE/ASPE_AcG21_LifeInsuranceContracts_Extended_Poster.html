ASPE Accounting Guideline AcG-21 — Accounting for Life Insurance Contracts with Cash Surrender Value
Extended reference poster · Canadian GAAP (ASPE — Part II of the CPA Canada Handbook) · The policyholder's own accounting for corporate-owned life insurance, a common Canadian estate planning tool · CAD examples
A NARROW, PRACTICALLY IMPORTANT CANADIAN CONTEXT: This Guideline addresses the POLICYHOLDER's own accounting for corporate-owned life insurance — not the insurer's accounting for contracts issued. It ties back to the same tax-planning and estate-structuring themes seen in the RoMRS relief (Section 3251) and Investment Tax Credits (Section 3805), since corporate-owned key-person life insurance is a genuinely common Canadian private-enterprise practice.

Conceptual Framework Linkage

Recognizing a genuine asset within a routine insurance premium
AcG-21 addresses HOW a POLICYHOLDER enterprise ACCOUNTS for a LIFE insurance POLICY it OWNS — TYPICALLY, corporate-OWNED life INSURANCE on a KEY employee OR shareholder — WHERE that POLICY carries a CASH surrender VALUE (CSV). THE core PRINCIPLE is RECOGNIZING the CSV as an ASSET, SEPARATE from the INSURANCE protection ELEMENT of the SAME premium PAYMENT (Section 4).
2

Scope — the Policyholder's Perspective

The company owning the policy, not the insurance company issuing it
This Guideline applies TO an ENTERPRISE that OWNS a LIFE insurance POLICY with a CASH surrender VALUE — ADDRESSING the POLICYHOLDER'S own ACCOUNTING, ENTIRELY separate FROM how an INSURANCE company WOULD account FOR contracts it ISSUES.
3

Why Private Enterprises Hold Corporate-Owned Life Insurance

Key-person protection and estate/tax planning
CORPORATE-owned LIFE insurance is a GENUINELY common CANADIAN private-ENTERPRISE practice — PROTECTING against the LOSS of a KEY employee OR shareholder, and OFTEN serving AS part of a BROADER estate OR tax-PLANNING structure (SIMILAR in SPIRIT to the ESTATE-freeze and RoMRS arrangements COVERED under SECTION 3251 — see the COMPANION poster). THE policy'S cash SURRENDER value REPRESENTS a GENUINE, realizable ASSET the COMPANY could ACCESS by SURRENDERING or BORROWING against the POLICY.
4

THE CORE PRINCIPLE — Recognizing Cash Surrender Value as an Asset

An asset distinct from the pure insurance protection element
THE policyholder RECOGNIZES the CASH surrender VALUE of the POLICY as an ASSET on ITS own BALANCE Sheet — REFLECTING the GENUINE, realizable VALUE the POLICY carries BEYOND its PURE insurance-PROTECTION function, DISTINCT from the PORTION of EACH premium payment THAT simply PAYS for the COST of insurance COVERAGE itself.
5

Allocating Premiums — Insurance Cost vs Cash Surrender Value Increase

Two components within a single premium payment
ComponentTreatment
Cost of insurance protectionEXPENSED as INCURRED — REFLECTING the GENUINE cost OF the underlying RISK coverage FOR the period
Increase in cash surrender valueCAPITALIZED, INCREASING the CSV asset ON the BALANCE Sheet
6

Changes in Cash Surrender Value

Recognized in net income as they occur
CHANGES in the POLICY'S cash SURRENDER value ARE recognized WITHIN net INCOME for the PERIOD — GIVEN ASPE'S absence OF any OCI mechanism (see the COMPANION Section 1520 poster), THERE is no ALTERNATIVE presentation AVAILABLE for THESE changes.
7

Worked Example — Allocating an Annual Premium Payment

Splitting a single payment into two distinct components
Example: A key-person policy premium
A private company pays a $45,000 annual premium on a key-person life insurance policy covering its founder. Of this amount, the policy's cash surrender value is determined to have increased by $28,000 during the year, with the remainder representing the cost of insurance protection.
The COMPANY expenses $45,000 − $28,000 = $17,000 as the COST of insurance PROTECTION, and CAPITALIZES the REMAINING $28,000 as an INCREASE to the CASH surrender VALUE asset ON its BALANCE Sheet.
8

Worked Example — Recognizing a Cash Surrender Value Increase

Tracking the asset's growth over time
Example: The policy's carrying value over successive years
Continuing the prior example, the policy's cash surrender value was $310,000 at the start of the year.
FOLLOWING the $28,000 INCREASE, the POLICY'S cash SURRENDER value ASSET is $338,000 at YEAR end — RECOGNIZED and CARRIED on the COMPANY'S Balance SHEET, REPRESENTING a GENUINE, realizable VALUE distinct FROM the pure INSURANCE protection ELEMENT of the ARRANGEMENT.

A Note on Scope — Policyholder, Not Insurer

A fundamentally different question from insurance contract accounting
THIS Guideline SHOULD not be CONFUSED with the BROADER question OF how an INSURANCE company ITSELF accounts FOR the insurance CONTRACTS it ISSUES (a SUBSTANTIALLY different, FAR more COMPLEX topic — SEE the COMPANION ASBE No. 25/26 POSTERS covering DIRECT insurance and REINSURANCE contracts EARLIER in this LIBRARY'S Chinese GAAP SERIES, for a SENSE of the SCALE of that SEPARATE topic). AcG-21 addresses ONLY the ORDINARY policyholder'S own, RELATIVELY narrow ACCOUNTING question.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Cash surrender value (CSV)
The realizable value a life insurance policy carries beyond its pure insurance-protection function, recognized as an asset by the policyholder.
Corporate-owned life insurance
A life insurance policy owned by a company, typically on a key employee or shareholder, often used for key-person protection or estate planning purposes.
