HGB §332 — Verletzung der Berichtspflicht (Breach of the Reporting Obligation)
Extended reference poster · Book 3, Section 2, Subsection 6 · The auditor's own criminal accountability, closing the triangle with §331/§331a · German commercial law
CLOSING THE ACCOUNTABILITY TRIANGLE: §331 criminalises company management falsifying the accounts; §331a criminalises falsely swearing to their accuracy. §332 is the third piece — criminalising the AUDITOR who should have caught the falsification but instead reports incorrectly, conceals findings, or issues a false opinion. It also continues the PIE-specific aggravation pattern this series has traced through §316a, §323, and §331a.

Conceptual Framework Linkage

The auditor's own criminal exposure — the last piece of the accountability structure
§331 (company management falsifying the underlying accounts), §331a (falsely swearing the Bilanzeid), and §332 (the auditor themselves reporting incorrectly or issuing a false opinion) together form a genuinely complete criminal-accountability structure — every party with a hand in producing and verifying the SAME documents this poster series has spent so long documenting faces its own, tailored criminal exposure if they deliberately corrupt the process.
HOW §332 MAPS TO GERMAN ACCOUNTING PRINCIPLES
Independent verification, given criminal teeth — the entire audit apparatus this series covered (§§316–324a) exists to give the public confidence in the accounts; §332 protects the INTEGRITY of that verification itself PIE-specific escalation, continuing a pattern — §332(2) Satz 2 and §332(3) apply the SAME heightened scrutiny for public-interest entities already seen in §316a and §323's tiered liability caps

Scope — Auditors and Their Assistants

A Sonderdelikt specifically for the audit function
§332 is confirmed to be a Sonderdelikt limited to the Abschlussprüfer (statutory auditor, established across the earlier Audit poster) OR their Gehilfe (assistant) — genuinely different personnel from §331's company management/supervisory board Täterkreis, or §331a's narrower capital-market-oriented legal-representative Täterkreis.
3

§332(1) — the Base Offence

Three distinct prohibited acts, one shared penalty
§332(1) criminalises an auditor or their assistant who: (1) reports INCORRECTLY on the result of the audit of any of the documents listed in Section 4 below; (2) CONCEALS significant circumstances in the Prüfungsbericht (§321, established in the earlier Audit poster); or (3) issues a content-wise INCORRECT Bestätigungsvermerk (§322) — punishable by imprisonment of up to THREE YEARS or a fine.

An Honest Flag — Conflicting Signals on §325(2a)

Genuine uncertainty worth naming rather than resolving with false confidence
The earlier §§325–329 poster corrected an EARLIER error by describing §325(2a) as a filing-mechanics provision — "dauerhafte Hinterlegung" (permanent deposit) for large companies' Jahresabschluss. Researching §332 now, the SAME §325(2a) cross-reference appears in a context — "ein Einzelabschluss nach §325 Abs. 2a," listed SEPARATELY from the ordinary Jahresabschluss in the same enumeration — that reads more naturally as referring to a DISTINCT type of individual statement, not merely a filing mechanic applied to the ordinary one. Rather than asserting confident precision in either direction, this poster flags the tension honestly: §325(2a)'s exact substantive character may be more nuanced than either earlier characterisation captured fully, and is worth dedicated verification against the current, complete statutory text if it matters for a specific application.
⚠️ This is a deliberate choice to flag uncertainty rather than compound an earlier correction with a new, equally unverified claim.

Worked Example — a Concealed Going-Concern Risk

Omitting a known, significant finding from the internal report
Example 1: An auditor who identifies but conceals a genuine going-concern risk
During an audit, the engagement team identifies clear, significant indicators of going-concern risk — the client is genuinely at risk of insolvency within the coming year — that would ordinarily need to be flagged prominently in the Prüfungsbericht under §321, established in the earlier Audit poster. The lead auditor, aware of this, deliberately OMITS the finding from the report to preserve the client relationship.
The deliberate concealment of this SIGNIFICANT circumstance in the Prüfungsbericht satisfies §332(1)'s second prohibited act, exposing the auditor to imprisonment of up to THREE years or a fine — REGARDLESS of whether the Bestätigungsvermerk itself was otherwise accurate.
7

§332(2) Sentence 1 — Aggravation for Consideration, Enrichment, or Harm

A corruption-adjacent aggravating circumstance
§332(2) sentence 1 raises the maximum penalty to FIVE YEARS' imprisonment (or a fine) where the auditor acts FOR CONSIDERATION (gegen Entgelt) — i.e., in exchange for payment beyond their ordinary audit fee — or with the INTENT to enrich themselves or a third party, or to HARM another party. This is a genuinely corruption-flavoured aggravation, targeting the scenario where a false report or opinion is effectively BOUGHT.
8

Worked Example — a Bribed Auditor

A payment specifically for a false opinion, not merely the ordinary audit fee
Example 2: A side payment in exchange for a clean opinion
An auditor, having identified material misstatements that would ordinarily require an eingeschränkter (qualified) or versagter (adverse) opinion under §322 (established in the earlier Audit poster), instead issues an UNQUALIFIED opinion after receiving a substantial side payment from the client's management, separate from the ordinary engagement fee.
The auditor's conduct satisfies BOTH §332(1)'s base offence (an incorrect Bestätigungsvermerk) AND §332(2) sentence 1's aggravation — having acted "gegen Entgelt" for the side payment — raising the maximum penalty to FIVE years' imprisonment rather than the base three-year maximum.
9

§332(2) Sentence 2 — a SEPARATE PIE-Specific Aggravation

The same five-year maximum, triggered by company TYPE rather than corrupt motive
§332(2) sentence 2 supplies a GENUINELY SEPARATE aggravating ground, reaching the SAME five-year maximum penalty — this time triggered simply by issuing a content-wise incorrect Bestätigungsvermerk for a public-interest entity (per the §316a definition established in the earlier Audit poster), with NO requirement of bribery, enrichment intent, or any corrupt motive at all. The heightened penalty attaches purely because of the SYSTEMIC significance of a PIE audit failure — continuing the exact same escalation pattern this series has now traced through §316a's own PIE definition and §323's own tiered liability caps.
10

Worked Example — a False Opinion on a PIE, No Bribery Required

Company type alone drives the heightened penalty
Example 3: A knowingly false opinion at a listed company, with no financial motive
An auditor at a listed, capital-market-oriented client (a PIE per §316a) issues an unqualified opinion they KNOW to be false, motivated purely by a desire to avoid a difficult client conversation and preserve the relationship — genuinely no bribery, side payment, or enrichment intent is involved at all.
DESPITE the absence of any corrupt motive, §332(2) sentence 2 STILL applies purely because the client is a PIE — the maximum penalty remains FIVE years, identical to the bribery scenario in Example 2, since this specific aggravation is triggered by company TYPE rather than by the auditor's underlying motivation.
11

§332(3) — the Leichtfertigkeit Tier for PIE Cases

A milder tier, specifically for the PIE scenario when intent cannot be established
§332(3) supplies a LESSER penalty — imprisonment up to TWO years — specifically for the §332(2) sentence 2 (PIE) scenario where the auditor's conduct amounts only to Leichtfertigkeit (heightened, reckless negligence, the SAME concept established in the earlier §331a poster) rather than genuine intent. This mirrors the SAME degree-of-fault tiering structure this series has now seen repeatedly — most directly parallel to §331a's own three-tier structure covered in the previous poster.
12

Worked Example — Contrasting Intent and Leichtfertigkeit for the Same PIE Failure

Identical false opinion, genuinely different consequences depending on the mental state
Example 4: Two auditors, same PIE client, different mental states
Auditor A at a listed PIE client KNOWS the opinion they're issuing is false and issues it anyway. Auditor B at a different listed PIE client fails to catch a material misstatement due to a genuinely reckless, systematic disregard of clear audit-evidence red flags — falling short of actual knowledge, but well beyond ordinary carelessness.
Auditor A faces up to FIVE years under §332(2) sentence 2 — genuine intent regarding a PIE opinion. Auditor B faces up to TWO years under §332(3) — Leichtfertigkeit for the SAME category of PIE failure, a genuinely lesser but still criminal consequence, distinct from the merely administrative consequences that would apply if Auditor B's conduct fell all the way down to ordinary negligence instead.

15

Relationship to IFRS

Auditor criminal liability sits entirely outside any accounting standard
Precisely as with §331 and §331a, IFRS itself has no equivalent — auditor criminal accountability is governed entirely by each jurisdiction's own separate criminal and audit-oversight law, never by an accounting standard itself. §332 completes the same "legal infrastructure beneath any accounting framework" pattern this series has now traced through bookkeeping obligations, retention periods, disclosure filing, and criminal accountability for both preparers and verifiers alike.
