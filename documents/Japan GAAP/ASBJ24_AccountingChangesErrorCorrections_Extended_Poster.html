Conceptual Framework Linkage

A landmark shift from catch-up profit adjustments to genuine restatement
ASBJ Statement No. 24 governs HOW an entity accounts for THREE distinct types of change: CHANGES in accounting POLICY, CHANGES in accounting ESTIMATES, and CORRECTIONS of PRIOR PERIOD errors — each requiring a DIFFERENT accounting treatment. This Statement represented a GENUINELY MAJOR reform of Japanese practice, moving decisively AWAY from an OLDER approach that recognised the CUMULATIVE effect of such changes ENTIRELY within CURRENT period profit or loss, TOWARD retrospective RESTATEMENT of prior periods — bringing J-GAAP much CLOSER to IAS 8 internationally (Section 6).
2

Scope — Three Categories of Change

Policy changes, estimate changes, and error corrections each treated differently
This Statement applies WHENEVER an entity CHANGES an accounting POLICY, REVISES an accounting ESTIMATE, or DISCOVERS an ERROR in PREVIOUSLY issued financial statements — establishing DISTINCT treatment rules for EACH of these THREE genuinely different situations.
3

Changes in Accounting Policy — Retrospective Application

Prior period comparatives are restated as if the new policy always applied
Where an entity CHANGES an accounting POLICY (e.g. switching inventory cost formulas), the CHANGE is applied RETROSPECTIVELY — RESTATING PRIOR PERIOD comparative financial statements AS IF the NEW policy had ALWAYS been applied, UNLESS doing so is IMPRACTICABLE, in which case the change is applied PROSPECTIVELY from the EARLIEST practicable date instead.
4

Changes in Accounting Estimates — Prospective Application

No restatement — the revised estimate applies only going forward
Where an entity REVISES an accounting ESTIMATE (e.g. a revised useful life for depreciation, or an updated allowance for doubtful accounts), the CHANGE is applied PROSPECTIVELY — affecting ONLY the CURRENT and FUTURE periods, with NO restatement of PRIOR periods — reflecting that estimate REVISIONS represent NEW, updated information becoming available over time, rather than a correction of something that was WRONG when originally determined.
5

Correction of Prior Period Errors — Retrospective Restatement

Prior period financial statements are corrected as if the error never occurred
Where a MATERIAL ERROR in PREVIOUSLY issued financial statements is DISCOVERED (e.g. a mathematical mistake, a misapplication of an accounting policy, or fraud), the correction is applied RETROSPECTIVELY — RESTATING the AFFECTED prior period financial statements AS IF the error had NEVER occurred, ensuring COMPARATIVE information presented is genuinely ACCURATE.
6

THE MAJOR REFORM — Moving Away From the Old Cumulative-Effect Approach

A fundamental shift in how Japanese practice treats the past
PRIOR to this Statement, JAPANESE practice HISTORICALLY recognised the CUMULATIVE EFFECT of an accounting policy CHANGE or ERROR correction ENTIRELY within CURRENT period profit or loss — a "CATCH-UP" adjustment lumping the ENTIRE historical impact into the year the CHANGE was made or the error DISCOVERED, WITHOUT restating prior periods' OWN reported figures. ASBJ Statement No. 24 represented a GENUINELY MAJOR reform, moving DECISIVELY toward RETROSPECTIVE restatement instead — a change with REAL practical consequences for how comparability across periods is achieved, and bringing J-GAAP MUCH CLOSER to IAS 8's internationally established approach.
7

Distinguishing a Change in Estimate From a Change in Policy or an Error

Genuine judgement is often required
DISTINGUISHING a genuine CHANGE in ESTIMATE (prospective) from a CHANGE in POLICY or an ERROR correction (both retrospective) can require SIGNIFICANT judgement — for example, a REVISED depreciation method MIGHT reflect a genuine change in POLICY, OR it might reflect a change in the ESTIMATED PATTERN of economic benefit consumption (an estimate change) — the CORRECT classification determines WHETHER prior periods are restated at all.
8

Worked Example — Retrospective Application of a Policy Change

Restating the prior year comparative
Example: Switching inventory cost formulas
A company switches its inventory costing method from weighted average to FIFO, effective for the current year. Under the NEW FIFO method, PRIOR year inventory and cost of sales would have been ¥15,000,000 lower.
The company RESTATES the PRIOR year's comparative financial statements to reflect the ¥15,000,000 adjustment AS IF FIFO had ALWAYS been applied — presenting BOTH the current and prior year using a CONSISTENT, comparable basis, rather than recognising the ENTIRE ¥15,000,000 as a single CATCH-UP adjustment in the CURRENT year (as OLDER Japanese practice would have done).
9

Worked Example — Prospective Application of an Estimate Change

No restatement, only a forward-looking change
Example: Revising an asset's useful life
A company originally estimated a machine's useful life at 10 years; after 4 years, it revises the estimate to 6 REMAINING years (a total of 10 years, unchanged from the ORIGINAL estimate but reflecting updated information), based on new information about wear patterns.
The company applies the REVISED estimate PROSPECTIVELY — spreading the machine's REMAINING carrying amount over the NEW 6-year remaining life GOING FORWARD, WITHOUT restating the PRIOR 4 years' depreciation figures, since this represents a genuine CHANGE IN ESTIMATE, not a policy change or error correction.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Retrospective application
Restating prior period comparative financial statements as if a new accounting policy had always applied, or as if a prior period error never occurred.
Prospective application
Applying a revised accounting estimate only to the current and future periods, without restating prior periods.
