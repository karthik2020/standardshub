Conceptual Framework Linkage

The divesting entity's own accounting, for Japan's distinctive corporate restructuring tools
ASBJ Statement No. 7 addresses accounting from the perspective of an entity DIVESTING (transferring) a business to another entity — via a CORPORATE SPLIT (会社分割), sale of business, or share-for-share exchange. It is the natural COMPANION to ASBJ Statement No. 21 (Business Combinations), which addresses the ACQUIRING entity's side of the SAME transaction — together, the two Statements provide a complete J-GAAP accounting framework for Japan's genuinely DISTINCTIVE corporate reorganisation techniques.
2

Scope

Entities transferring a business to another entity
This Statement applies to entities TRANSFERRING a business (or a portion of a business) to ANOTHER entity, whether through a corporate split, a sale of business assets/liabilities, or a share exchange arrangement.
3

Japan's Corporate Split System (会社分割) — the Backdrop

A distinctively Japanese legal restructuring mechanism
Japan's 2005 COMPANIES ACT (会社法) reform SIGNIFICANTLY expanded the "CORPORATE SPLIT" (会社分割) system — a LEGAL mechanism allowing a company to TRANSFER all or part of a business (including its associated assets, liabilities, contracts, and employees) to ANOTHER company (existing or newly created), often WITHOUT requiring individual asset-by-asset transfer procedures. This tool became WIDELY used for Japanese corporate group reorganisations, spin-offs, and joint venture formations — creating the NEED for dedicated accounting guidance addressing BOTH sides of such transactions.
4

The Core Question — Has the Divesting Entity's Investment Genuinely Changed?

Distinguishing a genuine sale from a continuing investment
The CENTRAL analytical question is whether the divesting entity has GENUINELY ended its economic involvement with the divested business, or whether — through the CONSIDERATION received (e.g. shares in the transferee) — it EFFECTIVELY retains an ONGOING investment in that same underlying business. This distinction DRIVES whether a full GAIN or LOSS is recognised, or whether recognition is LIMITED (or entirely withheld).
5

Consideration Received — Cash/Assets vs Shares in the Transferee

The form of consideration is the key analytical driver
Where the divesting entity receives CASH or OTHER ASSETS UNRELATED to the transferee's own equity, the transaction is generally treated as a genuine SALE — the divested business's carrying amounts are DERECOGNISED, and a GAIN or LOSS is recognised for the difference between consideration received and carrying amount. Where the divesting entity instead receives SHARES IN THE TRANSFEREE, the analysis becomes MORE NUANCED (Section 6), since the entity may still hold an ONGOING economic interest in the SAME underlying business, just through a DIFFERENT ownership vehicle.
6

Continuing Involvement — Partial or No Gain Recognition

Recognising a gain only to the extent of the genuinely divested portion
Where the divesting entity RECEIVES shares in the TRANSFEREE and, as a result, retains SIGNIFICANT INFLUENCE or JOINT CONTROL over the transferee (or the transferee remains, in substance, an affiliated entity), the transaction is viewed as ONLY PARTIALLY a genuine sale — a gain is recognised ONLY to the extent the underlying business has effectively been sold to GENUINELY OUTSIDE parties, with the REMAINING portion treated as a CONTINUING investment carried forward at its PRE-TRANSACTION carrying amount, rather than restated to fair value. If the divesting entity retains CONTROL or the transaction otherwise doesn't represent a genuine change in economic substance, NO gain may be recognised at all.
7

A Clean Break — Full Gain/Loss Recognition

Genuine sales to unrelated third parties
Where the divesting entity has NO continuing involvement or influence over the divested business AFTER the transaction (a genuine SALE to an UNRELATED third party, receiving cash or non-equity consideration), the FULL gain or loss is recognised — the divested business's carrying amounts are DERECOGNISED entirely, with the difference against consideration received flowing THROUGH the Statement of Income.
8

Worked Example — a Clean Divestiture to an Unrelated Third Party

Full gain recognition on a genuine sale
Example: Selling a business division for cash
A company sells a business division (carrying amount of net assets: ¥1,200,000,000) to an entirely unrelated buyer for ¥1,600,000,000 cash, with no continuing involvement in the divested business thereafter.
The company recognises a ¥400,000,000 GAIN (¥1,600,000,000 − ¥1,200,000,000) IMMEDIATELY in the Statement of Income — a genuine, clean divestiture with NO continuing economic interest retained.
9

Worked Example — Receiving Shares in the Transferee, Retaining Significant Influence

Only a partial gain, reflecting continuing involvement
Example: A corporate split into a joint venture
A company transfers a business division (carrying amount ¥800,000,000) via a corporate split into a NEW joint venture entity, receiving a 40% equity stake (SIGNIFICANT INFLUENCE, but not control) in the joint venture in return, with the joint venture's OTHER 60% held by a genuinely unrelated partner.
Since the company RETAINS significant influence over the SAME underlying business (now held through its 40% joint venture stake), a gain is recognised ONLY to the extent of the 60% GENUINELY divested to the unrelated partner — with the REMAINING 40% "interest" carried forward at its PROPORTIONATE historical carrying amount, rather than restated to fair value, reflecting the CONTINUING nature of that portion of the investment.

Relationship to IAS/IFRS

Comparison — J-GAAP vs IFRS
FeatureJ-GAAP (ASBJ Statement No. 7)IFRS
General principle — control-based derecognitionAssesses whether control/significant influence is retained over the SAME underlying businessIFRS 10's control-based derecognition test, broadly similar underlying logic
Corporate split (会社分割) as a distinct legal mechanismA genuinely JAPAN-SPECIFIC legal restructuring tool this Statement was specifically developed to addressNO direct equivalent legal construct — IFRS addresses the ECONOMIC substance generally, without a standard built around this SPECIFIC Japanese legal mechanism
The UNDERLYING economic principle — continuing involvement limits gain recognition — is broadly CONVERGED with IFRS's general approach to control and derecognition. The SPECIFIC legal TRIGGER (Japan's corporate split system) is a genuinely DISTINCTIVE feature of the Japanese corporate law landscape this Statement was built to address.

Quick-Reference Glossary

Corporate split (会社分割)
A Japanese Companies Act legal mechanism allowing a company to transfer all or part of a business to another company, often without individual asset-by-asset transfer procedures.
Continuing involvement
A divesting entity's retained control, significant influence, or joint control over a divested business, limiting or eliminating gain recognition on the transaction.
